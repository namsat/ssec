Chapter 04

Quantum statistics: photons, phonons and electrons

A solid contains about $10^{23}$ particles per cubic centimetre. What matters is how many of them occupy each energy level on average, and that depends only on whether the particles are bosons or fermions. Three gases cover most of solid-state physics: photons, phonons and electrons.

1. Counting states: three distributions

Consider levels $\varepsilon_i$ with degeneracy $g_i$ occupied by $n_i$ particles, with fixed $N=\sum n_i$ and $U=\sum n_i\varepsilon_i$. Equilibrium is the distribution $\{n_i\}$ that maximises the number of microstates $W$.

ParticlesMicrostates per levelMean occupation $f(\varepsilon)=n_i/g_i$
Distinguishable (classical)$W=N!\prod_i g_i^{n_i}/n_i!$$f_{MB}=e^{-(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}$
Identical bosons$W=\prod_i\dfrac{(n_i+g_i-1)!}{n_i!\,(g_i-1)!}$$f_{BE}=\dfrac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}-1}$
Identical fermions$W=\prod_i\dfrac{g_i!}{n_i!\,(g_i-n_i)!}$$f_{FD}=\dfrac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1}$
Derivation of the Fermi–Dirac distribution (Lagrange multipliers)

Each of the $g_i$ states holds 0 or 1 fermion, so the number of ways to choose the occupied ones is the binomial coefficient above. With Stirling's formula $\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n$:

$$\ln W=\sum_i\big[g_i\ln g_i-n_i\ln n_i-(g_i-n_i)\ln(g_i-n_i)\big].$$

Maximise $\ln W-\alpha\sum n_i-\beta\sum n_i\varepsilon_i$ with respect to each $n_i$:

$$-\ln n_i+\ln(g_i-n_i)-\alpha-\beta\varepsilon_i=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{n_i}{g_i}=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha+\beta\varepsilon_i}+1}.$$

Comparison with thermodynamics ($\dd U=T\dd S+\mu\,\dd N$, $S=k_B\ln W$) identifies $\beta=1/k_BT$ and $\alpha=-\mu/k_BT$. The Bose case is identical except that $\ln W$ contains $(n_i+g_i)\ln(n_i+g_i)$, which changes the sign in front of the 1.

∎

For $\varepsilon-\mu\gg k_BT$ both quantum distributions reduce to the classical one: the gas is non-degenerate when the occupation of every state is much less than one. Photons and phonons are not conserved, so their chemical potential is zero: $\expval n=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1)$ (Planck distribution).

2. The photon gas and the black-body problem

Standing electromagnetic waves in a cavity of volume $V$ have wavevectors on a grid of spacing $\pi/L$. Counting the points in a spherical shell, with two polarizations:

$$g(\nu)\,\dd\nu=\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}V\,\dd\nu .$$

Classical result. Equipartition assigns $k_BT$ to every mode: $u(\nu)=8\pi\nu^2k_BT/c^3$ (Rayleigh–Jeans), which agrees with experiment at low frequency but diverges when integrated: the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Planck's law
$$u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},\qquad B_\lambda(\lambda,T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}.$$
Derivation and consequences

Each mode is a quantum oscillator with energies $nh\nu$. Its mean energy above the ground state is

$$\expval E=\frac{\sum_nnh\nu\,e^{-nh\nu/k_BT}}{\sum_ne^{-nh\nu/k_BT}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln\frac{1}{1-e^{-\beta h\nu}}=\frac{h\nu}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1},$$

which is $h\nu$ times the Bose occupation with $\mu=0$. Multiplying by the mode density gives Planck's law. High-frequency modes cost more than $k_BT$ to excite and are frozen out.

Wien displacement. Setting $\partial B_\lambda/\partial\lambda=0$ with $x=hc/\lambda k_BT$ gives $x=5(1-e^{-x})$, $x=4.965$: $\lambda_{\max}T=2.898\times10^{-3}$ m·K.

Stefan–Boltzmann. $U/V=\int_0^\infty u\,\dd\nu=\frac{8\pi(k_BT)^4}{(hc)^3}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\dd x}{e^x-1}=\frac{8\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^3}T^4$, and the emitted power per unit area is $M=\frac c4\frac UV=\sigma T^4$ with $\sigma=5.67\times10^{-8}$ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

∎

3. The phonon gas and the heat capacity of solids

The $3N$ vibrational modes of a crystal are harmonic oscillators; their quanta are phonons (dispersion relations in Phonons). The energy is $U=\int\hbar\omega\,\expval{n(\omega)}\,g(\omega)\,\dd\omega$, and the result depends on the model for $g(\omega)$:

Einstein model

All modes have the same frequency $\omega_E$:

$$C_V=3Nk_B\Big(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\Big)^2\frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{(e^{\theta_E/T}-1)^2}.$$

Correct limits at high $T$ but exponential freeze-out at low $T$, faster than observed.

Debye model

Acoustic modes $\omega=v_sk$, so $g(\omega)=\dfrac{3V\omega^2}{2\pi^2v_s^3}$ up to a cut-off $\omega_D$ fixed by $\int_0^{\omega_D}g=3N$:

$$C_V=9Nk_B\Big(\frac T{\theta_D}\Big)^3\int_0^{\theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\dd x .$$

Limits of the Debye result: $C_V\to3Nk_B$ for $T\gg\theta_D$ (Dulong–Petit) and $C_V\to\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B(T/\theta_D)^3$ for $T\ll\theta_D$. The $T^3$ law has the same origin as Stefan's $T^4$ law: only modes with $\hbar\omega\lesssim k_BT$ are excited, and their number grows as $T^3$.

4. The free-electron gas

Conduction electrons in a metal are modelled as free fermions in a box. States are plane waves with $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ and density of states (per unit volume, both spins)

$$g(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt E .$$

At $T=0$ all states are filled up to the Fermi energy:

$$n=\int_0^{E_F}g(E)\,\dd E\ \Longrightarrow\ E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3},\qquad k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\quad v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m},\quad T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_B}.$$

For metals $E_F\sim2$–$12$ eV and $T_F\sim10^4$–$10^5$ K, so the gas is strongly degenerate at any temperature at which the metal is solid. The mean energy is $\frac35E_F$ per electron even at $T=0$.

Sommerfeld expansion and electronic heat capacity
$$\int_0^\infty H(E)f(E)\,\dd E\simeq\int_0^\mu H(E)\,\dd E+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2H'(\mu)+\dots$$ $$\mu(T)\simeq E_F\Big[1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}\Big(\frac{T}{T_F}\Big)^2\Big],\qquad C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T\,g(E_F)=\frac{\pi^2}{2}nk_B\frac{T}{T_F}=\gamma T .$$
Why the result is so small: a counting argument, then the expansion

Only electrons within $\sim k_BT$ of $E_F$ can find empty states to be excited into. Their number is $\sim g(E_F)k_BT$ and each gains $\sim k_BT$: $\Delta U\sim g(E_F)(k_BT)^2$, hence $C\sim2g(E_F)k_B^2T$, smaller than the classical $\frac32nk_B$ by a factor $\sim T/T_F\approx10^{-2}$. This resolved the puzzle of the missing electronic heat capacity.

Precisely: integrate by parts, $\int Hf\,\dd E=\int K(E)(-f')\,\dd E$ with $K=\int_0^EH$. Since $-f'$ is a narrow even peak around $\mu$, expand $K$ to second order around $\mu$; the first-order term vanishes by symmetry and $\int(E-\mu)^2(-f')\dd E=\frac{\pi^2}{3}(k_BT)^2$. Applying the result with $H=g$ (for $n$) and $H=Eg$ (for $U$) yields $\mu(T)$ and $C_{el}$.

∎

At low temperature the total heat capacity of a metal is $C=\gamma T+AT^3$; a plot of $C/T$ versus $T^2$ gives $\gamma$ as intercept and hence the density of states at the Fermi level.

6. Screening in the Fermi gas

A charge $Q$ placed in an electron gas attracts or repels mobile electrons, which rearrange and weaken its potential at long distance. In the Thomas–Fermi approximation the local electron density follows the local potential, $\delta n(\vb r)=g(E_F)\,e\,\phi(\vb r)$, and Poisson's equation becomes

$$\nabla^2\phi-k_{TF}^2\phi=-\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\delta(\vb r),\qquad k_{TF}^2=\frac{e^2g(E_F)}{\varepsilon_0}=\frac{3ne^2}{2\varepsilon_0E_F}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\phi(r)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}e^{-k_{TF}r}.$$
Derivation

In equilibrium the electrochemical potential is uniform: $E_F[n(\vb r)]-e\phi(\vb r)=E_F(n_0)$. For a weak potential, $\delta n=\frac{\partial n}{\partial E_F}e\phi=g(E_F)e\phi$. The induced charge density is $-e\,\delta n$, so Poisson's equation reads $\nabla^2\phi=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}[Q\delta(\vb r)-e^2g(E_F)\phi]$. Fourier transforming, $\phi(q)=Q/[\varepsilon_0(q^2+k_{TF}^2)]$, whose inverse transform is the Yukawa form. Equivalently the static dielectric function is $\varepsilon(q)=1+k_{TF}^2/q^2$. With $g(E_F)=3n/2E_F$ for free electrons one obtains the second expression.

∎

In metals the screening length $1/k_{TF}$ is about 0.05 nm: charges are screened within one atomic spacing, which is why the independent-electron picture works well. In a non-degenerate semiconductor the same argument with Boltzmann statistics gives the Debye length $L_D=\sqrt{\varepsilon k_BT/q^2n}$, typically 10–100 nm, which sets the width of space-charge regions. A sharper treatment (Lindhard) adds Friedel oscillations $\propto\cos(2k_Fr)/r^3$.

7. Photon–matter interaction

Consider two levels $E_1 < E_2$ with populations $N_1,N_2$ in a radiation field of spectral energy density $u(\nu)$ at $h\nu=E_2-E_1$. Three processes occur:

ProcessRatePhoton produced
Absorption$B_{12}\,u(\nu)\,N_1$one photon removed
Spontaneous emission$A_{21}\,N_2$random direction and phase
Stimulated emission$B_{21}\,u(\nu)\,N_2$identical to the stimulating photon
Einstein relations
$$g_1B_{12}=g_2B_{21},\qquad\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}}=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}.$$
Proof by detailed balance

In thermal equilibrium upward and downward rates balance: $B_{12}uN_1=A_{21}N_2+B_{21}uN_2$, with $N_2/N_1=(g_2/g_1)e^{-h\nu/k_BT}$. Solving for $u$:

$$u(\nu)=\frac{A_{21}/B_{21}}{\dfrac{g_1B_{12}}{g_2B_{21}}e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}.$$

This must equal Planck's law at every temperature, which requires both relations. Without stimulated emission one would obtain Wien's law instead of Planck's.

∎

The ratio of stimulated to spontaneous emission equals the photon occupation number $\expval n=1/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)$, tiny for visible light at ordinary temperatures. A beam crossing the medium changes as

$$\frac{\dd I}{\dd z}=-\alpha I,\qquad\alpha\propto\Big(N_1\frac{g_2}{g_1}-N_2\Big)B_{21}\frac{h\nu}{c}.$$

In equilibrium $\alpha>0$ (absorption). Population inversion, $N_2/g_2>N_1/g_1$, makes $\alpha$ negative: the medium amplifies light, the principle of the laser. In a semiconductor the condition becomes $E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}>h\nu\ge E_g$ (Bernard–Duraffourg), achieved in a forward-biased junction. The microscopic rates follow from Fermi's golden rule with the dipole matrix element, and selection rules ($\Delta l=\pm1$ in atoms; vertical transitions $\Delta\vb k\approx0$ in crystals) decide which transitions are allowed. Optical absorption in solids is developed in Phonons, optics, SC and its device uses in p–n junction.
