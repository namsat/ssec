Chapter 12

Quantum simulations of band structures

Every band diagram used in the previous chapters comes from solving a Schrödinger equation for electrons in a periodic potential. This chapter explains how that is done in practice and lets you do it: three solvers run in your browser, from a one-dimensional superlattice to the real band structure of silicon and to graphene nanoribbons.

1. The problem and the hierarchy of methods

We need the eigenvalues $E_n(\vb k)$ of $\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+U(\vb r)$ with $U$ periodic. Bloch's theorem reduces the infinite crystal to one unit cell and one wavevector at a time. Every numerical method then follows the same three steps:

  1. Choose a basis $\{\chi_j\}$ compatible with Bloch's theorem: plane waves $e^{i(\vb k+\vb G)\cdot\vb r}$, atomic orbitals, or a real-space grid.
  2. Build the Hamiltonian matrix $H_{ij}(\vb k)=\bra{\chi_i}\hat H\ket{\chi_j}$ (and the overlap matrix if the basis is not orthogonal).
  3. Diagonalize for each $\vb k$ along a path through the Brillouin zone and plot the eigenvalues.

Methods differ in where the potential comes from and in how much is computed from first principles.

2. Lab 1 — plane-wave method for a superlattice

For a potential of period $d$, expand the Bloch function in plane waves, $\psi_k(x)=\sum_nc_n\,e^{i(k+G_n)x}$ with $G_n=2\pi n/d$. The Schrödinger equation becomes the central equation, a matrix eigenvalue problem:

$$\sum_{n'}\Big[\frac{\hbar^2(k+G_n)^2}{2m^*}\delta_{nn'}+U_{n-n'}\Big]c_{n'}=E\,c_n,\qquad U_n=\frac1d\int_0^dU(x)\,e^{-iG_nx}\,\dd x .$$

The kinetic energy is diagonal and the potential enters through its Fourier coefficients. The sum must be truncated: one keeps the plane waves with kinetic energy below a cut-off $E_{cut}$. This single number controls the accuracy, and increasing it lowers the computed energies monotonically toward the exact ones (variational principle). A smooth potential converges with few plane waves; a sharp one, like a square barrier, needs many.

For a square barrier of height $V_0$ and width $b$: $U_0=V_0b/d$, $U_n=V_0\sin(n\pi b/d)/n\pi$. The example below is a GaAs/AlGaAs superlattice: the discrete levels of isolated wells broaden into minibands as the barriers get thinner, exactly as atomic levels broaden into bands in a crystal, but with a period, and therefore a Brillouin zone and band widths, chosen by the crystal grower.

3. Lab 2 — the empirical pseudopotential method for real semiconductors

Why pseudopotentials. The true crystal potential diverges at each nucleus and valence wavefunctions oscillate rapidly in the core region, to stay orthogonal to the core states. Representing those oscillations would take thousands of plane waves. The pseudopotential idea (Phillips and Kleinman): the orthogonality requirement acts like a repulsive potential that nearly cancels the strong attraction inside the core. Valence electrons can therefore be described by smooth pseudo-wavefunctions in a weak effective potential, with the same eigenvalues. A weak, smooth potential has only a few significant Fourier components.

Hamiltonian. Diamond and zincblende crystals are fcc with two atoms at $\pm\boldsymbol\tau$, $\boldsymbol\tau=\frac a8(1,1,1)$. Writing the potential as a sum of atomic pseudopotentials $v_1$, $v_2$:

$$H_{\vb G\vb G'}(\vb k)=\frac{\hbar^2|\vb k+\vb G|^2}{2m}\delta_{\vb G\vb G'}+V_S(|\Delta\vb G|)\cos(\Delta\vb G\cdot\boldsymbol\tau)+i\,V_A(|\Delta\vb G|)\sin(\Delta\vb G\cdot\boldsymbol\tau),\qquad\Delta\vb G=\vb G-\vb G',$$

with symmetric and antisymmetric form factors $V_{S,A}=\frac12(v_1\pm v_2)$. In units of $2\pi/a$ the shortest reciprocal vectors of the fcc lattice have $|\vb G|^2=3,4,8,11$. The structure factor $\cos(\Delta\vb G\cdot\boldsymbol\tau)$ vanishes for $|\Delta\vb G|^2=4$, and beyond 11 the form factors are negligible, so the whole band structure of silicon is fixed by three numbers: $V_3$, $V_8$, $V_{11}$. Compound semiconductors add three antisymmetric ones, $V^A_3$, $V^A_4$, $V^A_{11}$, which measure how different the two atoms are. Cohen and Bergstresser (1966) fitted them to measured optical transitions for fourteen semiconductors; their values are used here.

What to try
  • Compare Si (indirect gap, minimum along Γ–X at 85% of the way) with Ge (indirect, minimum at L) and GaAs (direct, at Γ).
  • Ge, GaAs and ZnSe are in the same row of the periodic table and share the same symmetric form factors. Select Ge and raise the ionicity slider, which switches on the antisymmetric potential: at 1 the crystal is GaAs, near 2.6 it is ZnSe. The gap widens and the minimum moves from L to Γ: the material turns from indirect to direct.
  • Reduce the number of plane waves and watch the bands shift: the same cut-off convergence as in Lab 1.
  • Change $V_3$ by 10% to see how sensitively the gap depends on the potential.

The computed band structure gives the quantities used in device models: the gap and whether it is direct, the positions of the conduction valleys, and, from the curvature at the extrema, the effective masses. The readout lists the Γ-valley electron mass obtained by finite differences of the computed $E(\vb k)$.

4. Lab 3 — tight binding for low-dimensional systems: graphene nanoribbons

Tight binding works in real space: list the atoms in one unit cell, connect neighbours with a hopping energy $-t$, and add a Bloch phase $e^{ikT}$ to bonds that cross the cell boundary. Nothing in the recipe requires periodicity in more than one direction, which makes it the natural tool for wires, ribbons and tubes:

$$H_{ij}(k)=-t\sum_{s=0,\pm1}\big[\text{atoms }i\text{ and }j+sT\text{ are neighbours}\big]\;e^{iksT}.$$

A graphene nanoribbon is a strip of the honeycomb lattice of Chapter 11. Transverse confinement quantizes the wavevector across the ribbon, so the ribbon bands are slices of the graphene cones; whether a slice passes through the Dirac point decides if the ribbon is metallic. The edge orientation matters:

  • Armchair ribbons with $N$ dimer lines: metallic for $N=3p+2$, semiconducting otherwise, with a gap that scales as $1/W$ (roughly $1\ \text{eV·nm}/W$). Three families alternate as $N$ grows.
  • Zigzag ribbons are always gapless in this model: a flat band at zero energy appears for $2\pi/3<|kT|\le\pi$, made of states localized on the edge atoms. In reality electron–electron interaction spin-polarizes the edges and opens a small gap.

5. First-principles calculations: density-functional theory

The empirical methods above take the potential as given. Density-functional theory (DFT) computes it.

Hohenberg–Kohn theorems and the Kohn–Sham equations

(1) The ground-state energy of interacting electrons in an external potential is a unique functional of the electron density $n(\vb r)$. (2) The true density minimises that functional. Kohn and Sham mapped the problem onto non-interacting electrons that reproduce the same density:

$$\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+v_{ion}(\vb r)+\underbrace{\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{n(\vb r')}{|\vb r-\vb r'|}\dd^3r'}_{v_H[n]}+\underbrace{\frac{\delta E_{xc}[n]}{\delta n(\vb r)}}_{v_{xc}[n]}\Big]\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i,\qquad n(\vb r)=\sum_{i}^{occ}|\phi_i(\vb r)|^2 .$$

All many-body effects are contained in the exchange–correlation functional $E_{xc}$, which is not known exactly. The local-density approximation (LDA) takes it from the uniform electron gas at the local density; generalized-gradient approximations (GGA, e.g. PBE) add the density gradient; hybrid functionals (HSE) mix in a fraction of exact exchange.

Because the potential depends on the density that it produces, the equations are solved by iteration:

  1. Guess a density (superposition of atomic densities).
  2. Build $v_H[n]$ and $v_{xc}[n]$; with a plane-wave basis $v_H$ is obtained from Poisson's equation by fast Fourier transform.
  3. Solve the Kohn–Sham equations at a grid of $\vb k$ points (this step is Lab 2 with a computed potential).
  4. Compute the new density, mix it with the old one, and repeat until the energy changes by less than a tolerance: the self-consistent field (SCF) cycle.
  5. With the converged potential, compute bands along any path, densities of states, forces on atoms and total energies.
Convergence checklist for any calculation
  • Plane-wave cut-off $E_{cut}$: raise it until total energy and gaps stop changing (Labs 1 and 2).
  • k-point mesh (Monkhorst–Pack grid) for Brillouin-zone integrals: metals need much denser meshes than insulators.
  • Pseudopotential (norm-conserving, ultrasoft or PAW): must be generated with the same functional and tested.
  • Geometry: relax atomic positions and lattice constants before computing bands.
  • Supercell size for surfaces, wires and defects: add enough vacuum or bulk that periodic images do not interact.

What DFT gets right and wrong. Lattice constants within about 1%, bulk moduli and phonon frequencies within a few percent, and the shape of valence bands are reliable. Kohn–Sham eigenvalues are not quasi-particle energies, however: LDA and GGA underestimate band gaps (silicon: 0.5–0.6 eV instead of 1.17 eV; germanium comes out metallic). Remedies are hybrid functionals, the $GW$ approximation, or, pragmatically, fitting an empirical model to a few experimental numbers. Widely used codes include Quantum ESPRESSO, ABINIT, VASP (plane waves), SIESTA (localized orbitals) and, for devices, tight-binding and non-equilibrium Green's-function simulators such as NEMO and Kwant.

Zone folding. A larger real-space cell means a smaller Brillouin zone, and the bands of the parent crystal are folded into it. This is why a supercell calculation shows many bands, why a silicon nanowire along [100] can acquire a direct gap (the X valleys fold onto Γ), and why the ribbon bands of Lab 3 are slices of the graphene dispersion. Confinement then shifts the folded bands by an amount of order $\hbar^2\pi^2/2m^*W^2$, raising the gap as the structure shrinks.
