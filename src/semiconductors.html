Chapter 07

Semiconductors: carriers, drift and diffusion

A semiconductor is useful because its carrier density can be set over ten orders of magnitude by doping, light or an applied voltage. This chapter counts carriers in equilibrium, describes how they move, and assembles the set of equations from which every device model in the following chapters is derived.

1. Bands, electrons, holes and the intrinsic concentration

Near the band edges the dispersion is parabolic, $E=E_c+\hbar^2k^2/2m_n^*$ for electrons and $E=E_v-\hbar^2k^2/2m_p^*$ for holes, with densities of states

$$g_c(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m_n^*}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt{E-E_c},\qquad g_v(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m_p^*}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt{E_v-E}.$$
Equilibrium carrier concentrations (non-degenerate)
$$n=N_c\,e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT},\qquad p=N_v\,e^{-(E_F-E_v)/k_BT},\qquad N_{c,v}=2\Big(\frac{2\pi m^*_{n,p}k_BT}{h^2}\Big)^{3/2}.$$ $$np=n_i^2=N_cN_v\,e^{-E_g/k_BT}\qquad\text{(mass-action law, independent of doping).}$$
Derivation

$n=\int_{E_c}^\infty g_c(E)f(E)\,\dd E$. If $E_c-E_F\gtrsim3k_BT$ the Fermi function reduces to $e^{-(E-E_F)/k_BT}$. With $x=(E-E_c)/k_BT$:

$$n=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m_n^*k_BT}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}\int_0^\infty\sqrt x\,e^{-x}\dd x,\qquad\int_0^\infty\sqrt xe^{-x}\dd x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2},$$

which is $N_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}$. The hole density follows in the same way from $1-f$. Multiplying, $E_F$ cancels. Without the Boltzmann approximation $n=N_c\mathcal F_{1/2}\big((E_F-E_c)/k_BT\big)$, needed when doping exceeds about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³.

∎

In an intrinsic crystal $n=p=n_i$ and the Fermi level sits at $E_i=\frac{E_c+E_v}{2}+\frac{k_BT}{2}\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c}$, close to midgap.

2. Doped semiconductors, band diagrams and the Shockley equations

A group-V atom (P, As) in silicon has one electron more than needed for bonding; it is bound like a hydrogen atom scaled by $m^*/\varepsilon_r^2$ (Atoms), with a level $E_d$ about 45 meV below $E_c$: a donor. A group-III atom (B) accepts an electron from the valence band, leaving a hole: an acceptor, 45 meV above $E_v$. At room temperature both are ionized.

Shockley equations
$$n=n_i\,e^{(E_F-E_i)/k_BT},\qquad p=n_i\,e^{(E_i-E_F)/k_BT}.$$

With charge neutrality $n+N_A^-=p+N_D^+$ and $np=n_i^2$:

$$n=\frac{N_D-N_A}{2}+\sqrt{\Big(\frac{N_D-N_A}{2}\Big)^2+n_i^2}\ \approx N_D-N_A,\qquad p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}\qquad(n\text{-type}).$$
Proof

Write the general result twice, for the doped and for the intrinsic material: $n=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_BT}$ and $n_i=N_ce^{-(E_c-E_i)/k_BT}$. Dividing eliminates $N_c$ and $E_c$: $n/n_i=e^{(E_F-E_i)/k_BT}$. The same for holes. Inserting $p=n_i^2/n$ in the neutrality condition gives a quadratic equation for $n$, with the root above.

∎

The Fermi level therefore measures the doping: $E_F-E_i=k_BT\ln(N_D/n_i)$ for n-type and $E_i-E_F=k_BT\ln(N_A/n_i)$ for p-type. Each decade of doping moves $E_F$ by $k_BT\ln10=60$ meV at 300 K. In a band diagram, the position of $E_F$ relative to $E_i$ tells at a glance which carrier is the majority.

3. Carrier concentration versus temperature

Including incomplete ionization, $N_D^+=N_D/\big[1+2e^{(E_F-E_d)/k_BT}\big]$ (the factor 2 is the spin degeneracy of the donor state), the neutrality equation must be solved numerically for $E_F$. Three regimes appear:

  • Freeze-out (low $T$): donors hold their electrons, $n\approx\sqrt{N_cN_D/2}\,e^{-(E_c-E_d)/2k_BT}$.
  • Extrinsic (saturation): $n\approx N_D$, independent of $T$. Devices are designed to operate here.
  • Intrinsic (high $T$): $n\approx n_i(T)\gg N_D$; doping no longer controls the material and junctions stop working. This sets the maximum operating temperature, higher for wide-gap materials (SiC, GaN).

4. Conduction by drift

Between collisions a carrier is accelerated by the field; after averaging over many collisions its mean velocity is proportional to the field at low fields:

$$\vb v_n=-\mu_n\vb E,\quad\vb v_p=\mu_p\vb E,\qquad\mu=\frac{q\tau}{m^*},\qquad\vb J_{drift}=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)\vb E=\sigma\vb E,\qquad\rho=\frac1\sigma .$$

The mobility is limited by scattering: lattice (phonon) scattering, $\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}$, and ionized-impurity scattering, $\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}/N$, combined by Matthiessen's rule $1/\mu=1/\mu_L+1/\mu_I$. Mobility therefore falls with doping. At high fields carriers emit optical phonons as fast as they gain energy and the velocity saturates at $v_{sat}\approx10^7$ cm/s:

5. Conduction by diffusion

Random thermal motion produces a net flux from regions of high to low concentration (Fick's law). For charged carriers:

$$\vb J_{n,diff}=qD_n\nabla n,\qquad\vb J_{p,diff}=-qD_p\nabla p .$$
Total current densities and Einstein relation
$$\vb J_n=q\mu_nn\vb E+qD_n\nabla n,\qquad\vb J_p=q\mu_pp\vb E-qD_p\nabla p,\qquad\frac{D}{\mu}=\frac{k_BT}{q}=V_T\ \ (25.85\text{ mV at 300 K}).$$
Proof of the Einstein relation

Consider a non-uniformly doped sample in equilibrium. No current flows: $J_n=q\mu_nnE+qD_n\frac{\dd n}{\dd x}=0$. In equilibrium $E_F$ is constant and the bands follow the electrostatic potential, $E_c(x)=E_{c0}-q\phi(x)$, so $n(x)=N_ce^{-(E_c(x)-E_F)/k_BT}\propto e^{q\phi/k_BT}$ and $\frac{\dd n}{\dd x}=\frac{qn}{k_BT}\frac{\dd\phi}{\dd x}=-\frac{qn}{k_BT}E$. Substituting: $q\mu_nnE-qD_n\frac{qn}{k_BT}E=0$ for every $E$, hence $D_n/\mu_n=k_BT/q$. The argument shows that non-uniform doping creates a built-in field $E=-V_T\,\frac{1}{n}\frac{\dd n}{\dd x}$, exploited in drift transistors.

∎

6. The mathematical model of semiconductors

Five coupled equations for the five unknowns $n$, $p$, $\vb J_n$, $\vb J_p$ and $\phi$ (with $\vb E=-\nabla\phi$):

Drift–diffusion model
$$\begin{aligned} &\text{Poisson:}&&\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\vb E)=q\,(p-n+N_D^+-N_A^-)\\ &\text{Transport:}&&\vb J_n=q\mu_nn\vb E+qD_n\nabla n,\qquad\vb J_p=q\mu_pp\vb E-qD_p\nabla p\\ &\text{Continuity:}&&\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}=\frac1q\nabla\cdot\vb J_n+G-U,\qquad\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac1q\nabla\cdot\vb J_p+G-U \end{aligned}$$

Generation and recombination. $G$ is the external generation rate (light). $U$ is the net recombination rate, which vanishes in equilibrium. The main mechanisms are:

  • Shockley–Read–Hall (via a trap level $E_t$, dominant in Si): $U=\dfrac{np-n_i^2}{\tau_p(n+n_1)+\tau_n(p+p_1)}$, with $n_1=n_ie^{(E_t-E_i)/k_BT}$, $p_1=n_i^2/n_1$. Midgap traps are the most effective.
  • Radiative (band to band, dominant in direct-gap materials): $U=B(np-n_i^2)$.
  • Auger (energy given to a third carrier, important at high density): $U=(C_nn+C_pp)(np-n_i^2)$.

Low-level injection. If the excess $\Delta n=\Delta p$ is much smaller than the majority concentration, every mechanism reduces to $U=\Delta p/\tau_p$ in n-type material (and $\Delta n/\tau_n$ in p-type): the minority-carrier lifetime.

Quasi-neutrality. A majority-charge imbalance disappears within the dielectric relaxation time $\tau_d=\varepsilon/\sigma$ ($\sim10^{-12}$ s), so in the bulk $\Delta n\approx\Delta p$ and the field is small. For minority carriers the drift term is then negligible and the model collapses to a single equation:

Minority-carrier diffusion equation (holes in n-type)
$$\frac{\partial\Delta p}{\partial t}=D_p\frac{\partial^2\Delta p}{\partial x^2}-\mu_pE\frac{\partial\Delta p}{\partial x}-\frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p}+G,\qquad L_p=\sqrt{D_p\tau_p}\ \ \text{(diffusion length)}.$$

7. Significant out-of-equilibrium cases

(a) Uniform illumination and photoconductive decay

With uniform generation there are no gradients: $\frac{\dd\Delta p}{\dd t}=G-\frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p}$. Steady state: $\Delta p=G\tau_p$, and the conductivity rises by $\Delta\sigma=q(\mu_n+\mu_p)G\tau_p$. When the light is switched off, $\Delta p(t)=G\tau_pe^{-t/\tau_p}$: measuring the decay of the photoconductivity gives the lifetime.

(b) Steady-state injection from one side

Excess holes are maintained at $x=0$ and removed at a contact at $x=W$ ($G=0$, $E=0$): $D_p\Delta p''=\Delta p/\tau_p$.

$$\Delta p(x)=\Delta p(0)\,\frac{\sinh[(W-x)/L_p]}{\sinh(W/L_p)}\ \longrightarrow\ \begin{cases}\Delta p(0)\,e^{-x/L_p}&W\gg L_p\ \text{(long sample)}\\[2pt]\Delta p(0)\,(1-x/W)&W\ll L_p\ \text{(short sample)}\end{cases}$$

The diffusion current at the injection plane is $J_p(0)=\frac{qD_p\Delta p(0)}{L_p}\coth\frac{W}{L_p}$. The long-sample case describes the neutral regions of a diode; the short-sample case describes the base of a bipolar transistor, where almost no carriers recombine in transit.

(c) The Haynes–Shockley experiment

A short light pulse creates $N_0$ excess holes per unit area at $x=0$, $t=0$ in an n-type bar with a uniform field $E$. The solution of the minority-carrier equation is a Gaussian that drifts, spreads and decays:

$$\Delta p(x,t)=\frac{N_0}{\sqrt{4\pi D_pt}}\exp\!\Big[-\frac{(x-\mu_pEt)^2}{4D_pt}\Big]\,e^{-t/\tau_p}.$$

A collector probe at distance $d$ records the pulse. Its arrival time gives $\mu_p=d/(Et_0)$, its width gives $D_p$, and its area gives $\tau_p$: one experiment measures mobility, diffusivity and lifetime of minority carriers and verifies the Einstein relation.

8. Quasi-Fermi levels

Out of equilibrium $np\ne n_i^2$ and a single Fermi level cannot describe both populations. Since carriers within each band thermalize in picoseconds, much faster than they recombine, one defines a separate level for each band:

$$n=n_i\,e^{(E_{Fn}-E_i)/k_BT},\qquad p=n_i\,e^{(E_i-E_{Fp})/k_BT},\qquad np=n_i^2\,e^{(E_{Fn}-E_{Fp})/k_BT}.$$
Currents and quasi-Fermi levels
$$J_n=\mu_nn\,\frac{\dd E_{Fn}}{\dd x},\qquad J_p=\mu_pp\,\frac{\dd E_{Fp}}{\dd x}.$$
Proof

From $n=n_ie^{(E_{Fn}-E_i)/k_BT}$: $\frac{\dd n}{\dd x}=\frac{n}{k_BT}\big(\frac{\dd E_{Fn}}{\dd x}-\frac{\dd E_i}{\dd x}\big)$, and the field is the slope of the bands, $qE=\frac{\dd E_i}{\dd x}$. Then $J_n=q\mu_nnE+qD_n\frac{\dd n}{\dd x}=\mu_nn\frac{\dd E_i}{\dd x}+\mu_nn\big(\frac{\dd E_{Fn}}{\dd x}-\frac{\dd E_i}{\dd x}\big)$ using $D_n=\mu_nk_BT/q$. Drift and diffusion combine into a single term.

∎

A gradient of a quasi-Fermi level is the driving force for that carrier's current; a flat quasi-Fermi level means no current. The splitting $E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}$ measures the departure from equilibrium: it equals $qV$ across a biased junction (next chapter), it is the maximum voltage a solar cell can deliver, and it must exceed the photon energy for optical gain.
