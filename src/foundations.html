Chapter 01

Foundations of quantum mechanics

Quantum theory keeps the structure of Hamiltonian mechanics and changes what the symbols mean: positions and momenta become operators, states become vectors, and predictions become probabilities. This chapter builds that dictionary step by step.

1. Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics

A mechanical system with generalized coordinates $q_i$ is described by the Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)=T-V$. The motion between two fixed configurations makes the action stationary:

$$S[q]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q,\dot q,t)\,\dd t,\qquad \delta S=0 .$$
Euler–Lagrange equations
$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0 .$$
Derivation from the principle of stationary action

Vary the path, $q_i\to q_i+\delta q_i$ with $\delta q_i(t_1)=\delta q_i(t_2)=0$:

$$\delta S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\delta q_i+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\delta\dot q_i\Big)\dd t .$$

Since $\delta\dot q_i=\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\delta q_i$, integrate the second term by parts. The boundary term vanishes because the endpoints are fixed, leaving

$$\delta S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}-\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i}\Big)\delta q_i\,\dd t=0 .$$

The variations $\delta q_i$ are arbitrary and independent, so the bracket must vanish for every $i$.

∎

Define the conjugate momentum $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ and the Hamiltonian by a Legendre transform, $H(q,p,t)=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L$. For $L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2-V(x)$ this gives $H=p^2/2m+V(x)$, the total energy. The equations of motion become first order:

$$\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad \dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.$$

For any function $f(q,p,t)$ on phase space,

$$\frac{\dd f}{\dd t}=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t},\qquad \{f,g\}=\sum_i\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\Big),$$

and the fundamental Poisson brackets are $\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}$. This bracket structure is what quantum mechanics inherits: $\{\cdot,\cdot\}\to\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\cdot,\cdot]$.

A free particle is therefore associated with a plane wave $e^{i(\vb k\cdot\vb r-\omega t)}$ with $\hbar\omega=\hbar^2k^2/2m$, and the wave equation that reproduces this dispersion relation is the Schrödinger equation.

Worked numbers

An electron accelerated through 100 V has $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2meV}=0.123$ nm, comparable to lattice spacings, which is why crystals diffract electrons. A thermal electron at 300 K in GaAs ($m^*=0.067m_0$) has $\lambda\approx 24$ nm: structures of that size show quantum confinement.

3. The postulates of quantum theory

  1. States. The state of a system is a normalized vector $\ket{\psi}$ of a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$. In the position representation, $\psi(\vb r,t)=\braket{\vb r}{\psi}$ is the wavefunction.
  2. Observables. Every measurable quantity $A$ corresponds to a Hermitian operator $\hat A$. Position and momentum satisfy $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$; in the position representation $\hat p=-i\hbar\,\partial_x$.
  3. Measurement outcomes. A measurement of $A$ yields one of the eigenvalues $a_n$ of $\hat A$, where $\hat A\ket{a_n}=a_n\ket{a_n}$.
  4. Born rule. The probability of the result $a_n$ is $P(a_n)=|\braket{a_n}{\psi}|^2$. For continuous spectra, $|\psi(x)|^2\dd x$ is the probability of finding the particle in $\dd x$.
  5. State reduction. Immediately after the result $a_n$, the state is the projection of $\ket\psi$ on the eigenspace of $a_n$ (normalized).
  6. Time evolution. Between measurements the state obeys the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\psi}=\hat H\ket{\psi}$.

The expectation value follows from the Born rule: $\expval{A}=\sum_n a_nP(a_n)=\bra{\psi}\hat A\ket{\psi}$.

4. Hilbert space and Dirac notation

A Hilbert space is a complete complex vector space with a scalar product $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ that is linear in $\ket\psi$, antilinear in $\ket\phi$, and satisfies $\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\braket{\psi}{\phi}^*$ and $\braket{\psi}{\psi}\ge0$. For wavefunctions,

$$\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\int\phi^*(x)\,\psi(x)\,\dd x .$$

A ket $\ket\psi$ is a vector; a bra $\bra\phi$ is the linear functional that maps $\ket\psi$ to $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$. An orthonormal basis $\{\ket{n}\}$ satisfies $\braket{m}{n}=\delta_{mn}$ and the completeness relation

$$\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=\hat 1,\qquad\text{or for continuous bases}\qquad\int\dd x\,\ket{x}\bra{x}=\hat1,\quad\braket{x}{x'}=\delta(x-x').$$

Inserting the identity turns abstract statements into components: $\ket\psi=\sum_n\ket n\braket{n}{\psi}=\sum_n c_n\ket n$, and an operator becomes a matrix $A_{mn}=\bra m\hat A\ket n$. Changing from position to momentum representation uses $\braket{x}{p}=e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, i.e. a Fourier transform.

Adjoint and Hermitian operators

The adjoint $\hat A^\dagger$ is defined by $\bra\phi\hat A^\dagger\ket\psi=\bra\psi\hat A\ket\phi^*$. An operator is Hermitian if $\hat A^\dagger=\hat A$, and unitary if $\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat 1$.

Theorem: spectral properties of Hermitian operators

(i) The eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real. (ii) Eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal. (iii) The eigenvectors form a complete set, so $\hat A=\sum_n a_n\ket{a_n}\bra{a_n}$.

Proof of (i) and (ii)

Let $\hat A\ket{a}=a\ket a$ and $\hat A\ket{b}=b\ket b$. Then $\bra b\hat A\ket a=a\braket{b}{a}$. Using Hermiticity, $\bra b\hat A\ket a=\bra a\hat A\ket b^*=b^*\braket{a}{b}^*=b^*\braket{b}{a}$. Subtracting,

$$(a-b^*)\braket{b}{a}=0 .$$

With $\ket b=\ket a$: $\braket{a}{a}>0$ so $a=a^*$, the eigenvalue is real. With $a\neq b$ (both now real): $\braket{b}{a}=0$.

∎

Theorem: compatible observables

Two Hermitian operators have a common complete set of eigenvectors if and only if they commute, $[\hat A,\hat B]=0$. Such observables can be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision. A complete set of commuting observables (CSCO) labels each basis state uniquely, e.g. $\{\hat H,\hat L^2,\hat L_z,\hat S_z\}$ for hydrogen.

5. The Heisenberg uncertainty relation

Define the spread $\Delta A=\sqrt{\expval{A^2}-\expval{A}^2}$.

Theorem (Robertson)
$$\Delta A\,\Delta B\ \ge\ \tfrac12\big|\expval{[\hat A,\hat B]}\big|\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}.$$
Proof with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

Let $\hat a=\hat A-\expval A$, $\hat b=\hat B-\expval B$ and define $\ket f=\hat a\ket\psi$, $\ket g=\hat b\ket\psi$. Then $(\Delta A)^2=\braket ff$, $(\Delta B)^2=\braket gg$, and Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge|\braket fg|^2\ge\big(\operatorname{Im}\braket fg\big)^2=\Big|\frac{\braket fg-\braket gf}{2i}\Big|^2 .$$

But $\braket fg-\braket gf=\bra\psi[\hat a,\hat b]\ket\psi=\expval{[\hat A,\hat B]}$, since constants commute. Taking the square root proves the statement. Equality requires $\ket g=i\lambda\ket f$ with real $\lambda$, which for $x$ and $p$ gives a Gaussian wave packet.

∎

The relation is not a statement about clumsy instruments: no state exists in which both spreads are small. A Gaussian packet $\psi\propto e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}e^{ik_0x}$ has $\Delta x=\sigma$ and $\Delta k=1/2\sigma$, the minimum allowed.

6. The Schrödinger equation and time evolution

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\vb r,t)\Big]\psi .$$

If $V$ does not depend on time, separation of variables $\psi=\varphi(\vb r)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ gives the time-independent equation $\hat H\varphi_n=E_n\varphi_n$. These stationary states have time-independent probability density. The general solution is a superposition

$$\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_n c_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\ket{n},\qquad c_n=\braket{n}{\psi(0)},$$

or, formally, $\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}$ with the unitary operator $\hat U=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$. Unitarity preserves the norm, so total probability is conserved. Locally:

Continuity equation
$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\vb J=0,\qquad\rho=|\psi|^2,\quad\vb J=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\big(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*\big)=\operatorname{Re}\Big(\psi^*\frac{\hat{\vb p}}{m}\psi\Big).$$
Proof

$\partial_t(\psi^*\psi)=\psi^*\partial_t\psi+\psi\,\partial_t\psi^*$. Insert $\partial_t\psi=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\big(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V\psi\big)$ and its complex conjugate. For real $V$ the potential terms cancel and

$$\partial_t\rho=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\psi^*\nabla^2\psi-\psi\nabla^2\psi^*\big)=-\nabla\cdot\Big[\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\big(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*\big)\Big].$$

∎

For a plane wave $Ae^{ikx}$ the current is $J=|A|^2\hbar k/m$: density times velocity. This is the quantity used to define transmission and reflection coefficients in scattering problems.

7. The Ehrenfest theorem

Theorem
$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\expval{A}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\expval{[\hat A,\hat H]}+\Big\langle\frac{\partial\hat A}{\partial t}\Big\rangle .$$

For $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V(\hat x)$: $\ \dfrac{\dd\expval x}{\dd t}=\dfrac{\expval p}{m},\qquad\dfrac{\dd\expval p}{\dd t}=-\Big\langle\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial x}\Big\rangle .$

Proof

Differentiate $\expval A=\bra\psi\hat A\ket\psi$ and use $i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket\psi=\hat H\ket\psi$, $-i\hbar\,\partial_t\bra\psi=\bra\psi\hat H$:

$$\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\expval A=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\bra\psi(\hat A\hat H-\hat H\hat A)\ket\psi+\bra\psi\partial_t\hat A\ket\psi .$$

For the two special cases use $[\hat x,\hat p^2]=2i\hbar\hat p$ and $[\hat p,V(\hat x)]=-i\hbar V'(\hat x)$.

∎

Mean values obey Newton-like equations, which is the classical limit: if the packet is narrow compared with the scale on which $V'$ varies, $\expval{V'(x)}\approx V'(\expval x)$ and the centre of the packet follows the classical trajectory. Comparing with $\dot f=\{f,H\}$ shows the rule behind canonical quantization:

Canonical quantization

Write the classical Hamiltonian in canonical variables, replace $q_i,p_i$ by Hermitian operators, and impose $[\hat q_i,\hat p_j]=i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$, that is, $\{\cdot,\cdot\}\to\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\cdot,\cdot]$. Products of non-commuting variables must be symmetrized. The same recipe applied to each mode of the electromagnetic field produces photons (see Atoms).

8. Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures

Only matrix elements $\bra\phi\hat A\ket\psi$ are observable, so the time dependence can be assigned to states or to operators.

Schrödinger pictureHeisenberg picture
States$\ket{\psi_S(t)}=\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}$$\ket{\psi_H}=\ket{\psi(0)}$, fixed
Operators$\hat A_S$ fixed$\hat A_H(t)=\hat U^\dagger(t)\hat A_S\hat U(t)$
Equation of motion$i\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\psi_S}=\hat H\ket{\psi_S}$$i\hbar\,\dfrac{\dd\hat A_H}{\dd t}=[\hat A_H,\hat H]+i\hbar\,\partial_t\hat A_H$

Heisenberg's equation is the operator version of $\dot f=\{f,H\}$. For the harmonic oscillator it gives $\dot{\hat x}=\hat p/m$, $\dot{\hat p}=-m\omega^2\hat x$, with the classical solution $\hat x(t)=\hat x(0)\cos\omega t+\frac{\hat p(0)}{m\omega}\sin\omega t$. The interaction picture, in which operators evolve with $\hat H_0$ and states with the perturbation, is the starting point of time-dependent perturbation theory and Fermi's golden rule:

$$\Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|\bra f\hat H'\ket i|^2\,\rho(E_f).$$
