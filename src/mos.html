Chapter 10

MOS systems and the MOSFET

A metal–oxide–semiconductor capacitor lets a gate voltage control the charge at a semiconductor surface without drawing current. Add a source and a drain and the induced charge becomes a channel: the MOSFET, the device produced in the largest numbers in history.

Convention: p-type substrate (n-channel device), doping $N_A$, oxide thickness $t_{ox}$, $C_{ox}=\varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$ per unit area, $\phi_F=V_T\ln(N_A/n_i)$, and $\psi_s$ the surface potential (band bending, positive when bands bend down). The thermal voltage is written $V_T=k_BT/q$ and the threshold voltage $V_{TH}$.

1. The MOS system: band diagram at equilibrium

Three materials are aligned to a common vacuum level: the gate with work function $\Phi_M$, the oxide (SiO₂: gap 9 eV, barrier of 3.1 eV for electrons from silicon), and the semiconductor with electron affinity $\chi=4.05$ eV and work function $\Phi_S=\chi+E_g/2q+\phi_F$. When gate and substrate are connected, the Fermi levels align and the work-function difference $\phi_{ms}=\Phi_M-\Phi_S$ drops partly across the oxide and partly as band bending in the silicon.

Flat-band voltage

The gate voltage that makes the bands flat ($\psi_s=0$, no charge in the semiconductor):

$$V_{FB}=\phi_{ms}-\frac{Q_{ox}}{C_{ox}},$$

where $Q_{ox}$ is the effective fixed positive charge at the Si–SiO₂ interface (plus mobile ions and trapped charge, weighted by position). For an n⁺ polysilicon gate on p-Si, $\phi_{ms}\simeq-(E_g/2q+\phi_F)\approx-0.9$ to $-1.0$ V.

For any gate voltage the potential divides between oxide and semiconductor, and charge neutrality links gate charge and semiconductor charge:

$$V_G=V_{FB}+\psi_s+V_{ox},\qquad V_{ox}=-\frac{Q_s(\psi_s)}{C_{ox}} .$$

Inversion of the carrier population. The surface concentrations follow the band bending, $n_s=n_{p0}e^{\psi_s/V_T}$ and $p_s=N_Ae^{-\psi_s/V_T}$. When $\psi_s=\phi_F$ the intrinsic level crosses the Fermi level at the surface and $n_s=p_s=n_i$; beyond that, electrons are the majority at the surface of a p-type crystal. At $\psi_s=2\phi_F$, $n_s=N_A$. The electrons of the inversion layer are confined within a few nanometres of the interface and are supplied by thermal generation (slowly, in a capacitor) or by the source and drain (instantly, in a transistor).

3. Total charge in the semiconductor

Exact charge–potential relation
$$Q_s(\psi_s)=\mp\sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_AV_T}\;\left[\Big(e^{-\psi_s/V_T}+\frac{\psi_s}{V_T}-1\Big)+\frac{n_i^2}{N_A^2}\Big(e^{\psi_s/V_T}-\frac{\psi_s}{V_T}-1\Big)\right]^{1/2}$$

(negative for $\psi_s>0$). The prefactor equals $\sqrt2\,\varepsilon_sV_T/L_D$ with the Debye length $L_D=\sqrt{\varepsilon_sV_T/qN_A}$.

Derivation: first integral of the Poisson–Boltzmann equation

In the semiconductor $p=N_Ae^{-\psi/V_T}$, $n=\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}e^{\psi/V_T}$, so

$$\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_s}=-\frac{q}{\varepsilon_s}\Big[N_A\big(e^{-\psi/V_T}-1\big)-\frac{n_i^2}{N_A}\big(e^{\psi/V_T}-1\big)\Big],$$

where the $-1$ terms come from the fixed dopant charge (neutral bulk at $\psi=0$). Multiply by $2\,\dd\psi/\dd x$ and use $2\psi'\psi''=\frac{\dd}{\dd x}(\psi')^2$; integrate from the bulk ($\psi=0$, $\psi'=0$) to the surface:

$$E_s^2=\Big(\frac{\dd\psi}{\dd x}\Big)^2_{x=0}=\frac{2qN_AV_T}{\varepsilon_s}\Big[\Big(e^{-\psi_s/V_T}+\frac{\psi_s}{V_T}-1\Big)+\frac{n_i^2}{N_A^2}\Big(e^{\psi_s/V_T}-\frac{\psi_s}{V_T}-1\Big)\Big].$$

Gauss's law at the surface, $Q_s=-\varepsilon_sE_s$, gives the result.

∎

Each regime corresponds to one dominant term: $e^{-\psi_s/V_T}$ in accumulation, $\psi_s/V_T$ in depletion (the familiar $\sqrt{\psi_s}$ law), and $(n_i^2/N_A^2)e^{\psi_s/V_T}$ in strong inversion. Because the inversion charge grows exponentially with $\psi_s$, a tiny increase of band bending supplies any charge the gate demands: the surface potential is pinned near $2\phi_F$ and the depletion width saturates at

$$W_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s(2\phi_F)}{qN_A}} .$$ {{fig|qs|Semiconductor charge and surface potential|Left: magnitude of the semiconductor charge versus surface potential on a logarithmic scale. 4. Strong inversion and the threshold voltage

Threshold voltage
$$V_{TH}=V_{FB}+2\phi_F+\frac{\sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_A(2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}}=V_{FB}+2\phi_F+\gamma\sqrt{2\phi_F},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_A}}{C_{ox}}.$$

Above threshold the inversion charge is $Q_n\simeq-C_{ox}(V_G-V_{TH})$.

Derivation

Define threshold as the onset of strong inversion, $\psi_s=2\phi_F$. At that point the inversion charge is still negligible compared with the depletion charge $Q_B=-qN_AW_{\max}=-\sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_A(2\phi_F)}$. Insert in $V_G=V_{FB}+\psi_s-Q_s/C_{ox}$ to obtain $V_{TH}$.

For $V_G>V_{TH}$, $\psi_s$ and $Q_B$ stay practically constant, so the additional gate charge is balanced by inversion charge only: $Q_n=Q_s-Q_B=-C_{ox}(V_G-V_{FB}-2\phi_F)-Q_B=-C_{ox}(V_G-V_{TH})$. The MOS structure behaves as a parallel-plate capacitor between the gate and the inversion layer.

∎

$V_{TH}$ is set in fabrication by the gate material ($\phi_{ms}$), the oxide thickness, the substrate doping and, most flexibly, by a shallow threshold-adjust implant of dose $D_I$, which shifts it by $\pm qD_I/C_{ox}$.

MOS capacitance. The small-signal capacitance is the series of $C_{ox}$ and $C_s=-\dd Q_s/\dd\psi_s$. In accumulation $C\to C_{ox}$; in depletion $C$ falls as the depletion layer widens; at flat band $C_{FB}=\big(1/C_{ox}+L_D/\varepsilon_s\big)^{-1}$. In inversion the result depends on frequency: at low frequency the inversion layer follows the signal and $C\to C_{ox}$ again; at high frequency minority carriers cannot be generated fast enough and $C$ stays at $C_{\min}=\big(1/C_{ox}+W_{\max}/\varepsilon_s\big)^{-1}$. The C–V curve is the standard tool for extracting $t_{ox}$, $N_A$, $V_{FB}$ and oxide and interface charges.

5. The MOSFET: gradual-channel analysis

An n-channel MOSFET adds n⁺ source and drain regions at the two ends of the gate. With $V_{GS}>V_{TH}$ an inversion layer connects them; a drain voltage drives electrons from source to drain by drift. A p-channel device is the complement: n-type body, p⁺ source and drain, holes in the channel, all voltages and currents reversed, $V_{TH}<0$.

Gradual-channel approximation (Shockley): the field along the channel is much smaller than the vertical field, so at each position $y$ the one-dimensional MOS solution applies with the local channel potential $V(y)$, which rises from 0 at the source to $V_{DS}$ at the drain.

Long-channel drain current
$$I_D=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac WL\Big[(V_{GS}-V_{TH})V_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\Big]\quad(V_{DS}\le V_{GS}-V_{TH}),\qquad I_D=\frac{\mu_nC_{ox}}{2}\frac WL(V_{GS}-V_{TH})^2\quad(V_{DS}\ge V_{GS}-V_{TH}).$$
Derivation

At position $y$ the inversion charge per unit area is $Q_n(y)=-C_{ox}[V_{GS}-V_{TH}-V(y)]$ (neglecting the variation of the depletion charge along the channel). The current is drift of this sheet of charge in the lateral field $-\dd V/\dd y$:

$$I_D=W\mu_n|Q_n(y)|\frac{\dd V}{\dd y}=W\mu_nC_{ox}\big[V_{GS}-V_{TH}-V\big]\frac{\dd V}{\dd y}.$$

$I_D$ is the same at every $y$ (current continuity). Integrate from $y=0$, $V=0$ to $y=L$, $V=V_{DS}$:

$$I_DL=W\mu_nC_{ox}\Big[(V_{GS}-V_{TH})V_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\Big].$$

The parabola has its maximum at $V_{DS,sat}=V_{GS}-V_{TH}$, where $Q_n(L)=0$: the channel is pinched off at the drain. Beyond this the gradual-channel solution is not valid near the drain; the voltage in excess of $V_{DS,sat}$ drops across a short depleted region and the current stays at its maximum value, the saturation current.

Including the dependence of the depletion charge on $V(y)$ gives the more accurate expression $I_D=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac WL\big\{(V_{GS}-V_{FB}-2\phi_F-\frac{V_{DS}}2)V_{DS}-\frac23\gamma[(2\phi_F+V_{DS})^{3/2}-(2\phi_F)^{3/2}]\big\}$, usually approximated by replacing $V_{DS}^2/2$ with $mV_{DS}^2/2$, $m=1+\gamma/2\sqrt{2\phi_F}\approx1.1$–$1.4$.

∎

with $k_n=\mu_nC_{ox}W/L$. Channel-length modulation: in saturation the pinch-off point moves toward the source as $V_{DS}$ grows, shortening the effective channel, $I_D\propto1/(L-\Delta L)$; this is modelled by the factor $1+\lambda V_{DS}$ with $\lambda\propto1/L$.

Body (substrate) effect
$$V_{TH}=V_{TH0}+\gamma\Big(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F}\Big).$$

A reverse bias $V_{SB}$ between source and body widens the depletion region under the channel; more gate charge is needed to balance it before inversion sets in.

The body acts as a second, weaker gate. The effect matters whenever the source is not tied to the body, as in stacked transistors and source followers.

Small-signal model (saturation). Linearizing around the bias point:

$$g_m=\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}=k_n(V_{GS}-V_{TH})=\sqrt{2k_nI_D}=\frac{2I_D}{V_{GS}-V_{TH}},\qquad g_{ds}=\frac1{r_o}=\lambda I_D,\qquad g_{mb}=\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{BS}}=g_m\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}}.$$

The circuit is a voltage-controlled current source $g_mv_{gs}$ (plus $g_{mb}v_{bs}$) in parallel with $r_o$, with an open-circuit gate at dc and the capacitances above. The transition frequency is

$$f_T=\frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_{gs}+C_{gd})}\simeq\frac{3\mu_n(V_{GS}-V_{TH})}{4\pi L^2}\ \ \xrightarrow{\ \text{velocity saturation}\ }\ \ \frac{v_{sat}}{2\pi L}.$$

Compared with the BJT ($g_m=I_C/V_T$), the MOSFET has a lower transconductance at equal current, $g_m/I_D=2/(V_{GS}-V_{TH})$, bounded by $1/nV_T$ in weak inversion, but infinite dc input resistance and simpler scaling.

8. CMOS: complementary n- and p-channel transistors

CMOS technology builds both device types on the same wafer: n-channel transistors in p-type regions and p-channel transistors in n-wells. Hole mobility is 2–3 times lower than electron mobility, so p-channel devices are made wider for equal drive. In the CMOS inverter the two gates are tied to the input and the two drains to the output. For a logic-low input the pMOS conducts and pulls the output to $V_{DD}$; for a logic-high input the nMOS pulls it to ground. In either steady state one transistor is off, so the static current is only leakage: this is the reason CMOS displaced all other logic families.

9. Short-channel effects

When the channel length becomes comparable to the depletion widths of source and drain, the one-dimensional picture fails.

  • Threshold roll-off and DIBL. Part of the depletion charge under the gate is balanced by the source and drain junctions, not by the gate (charge sharing), so $V_{TH}$ falls as $L$ decreases. A high drain voltage lowers the source barrier further (drain-induced barrier lowering): $V_{TH}=V_{TH0}-\eta_{DIBL}V_{DS}$, typically 50–150 mV/V.
  • Subthreshold conduction. Below threshold the current is diffusive, $I_D\propto e^{(V_{GS}-V_{TH})/nV_T}$, with swing $S=nV_T\ln10\ge60$ mV/decade at 300 K, $n=1+C_{dep}/C_{ox}$. Since $S$ does not scale, neither can $V_{TH}$ without increasing the off current: the central limit on supply-voltage scaling.
  • Velocity saturation. For fields above $E_c\approx v_{sat}/\mu$ the carriers stop accelerating: $I_{D,sat}\to WC_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_{TH})v_{sat}$, linear instead of quadratic in gate overdrive and independent of $L$; $V_{DS,sat}$ becomes smaller than $V_{GS}-V_{TH}$.
  • Mobility degradation. The vertical field presses carriers against the interface: $\mu_{eff}=\mu_0/[1+\theta(V_{GS}-V_{TH})]$.
  • Hot carriers and impact ionization near the drain: substrate current, oxide charging and long-term degradation; mitigated with lightly doped drain extensions.
  • Punch-through, gate tunnelling, GIDL: leakage paths through the body, through the nanometre-thin oxide (hence high-κ dielectrics) and at the gate–drain overlap.

Electrostatic integrity is measured by the natural length $\lambda_c=\sqrt{(\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_{ox})\,t_{ox}\,t_{dep}}$: short-channel effects are under control when $L\gtrsim5\lambda_c$. Thinner oxides, shallower junctions and thinner bodies reduce $\lambda_c$, which explains the evolution from planar bulk devices to FinFETs and gate-all-around nanosheets, where the gate surrounds a thin silicon body.

10. Floating-gate devices and non-volatile memories

A floating-gate transistor has a second polysilicon gate completely surrounded by oxide between the control gate and the channel. Charge placed on it is trapped behind 3.1 eV barriers and stays for years without power. Seen from the control gate, stored charge $Q_{FG}$ shifts the threshold:

$$\Delta V_{TH}=-\frac{Q_{FG}}{C_{CG}},\qquad V_{FG}=\alpha_GV_{CG}+\frac{Q_{FG}}{C_{tot}},\qquad\alpha_G=\frac{C_{CG}}{C_{tot}}\approx0.5\text{–}0.7 .$$

Stored electrons ($Q_{FG}<0$) raise $V_{TH}$. Reading applies a control-gate voltage between the two thresholds: an erased cell conducts (“1”), a programmed cell does not (“0”). With several charge levels one cell stores 2, 3 or 4 bits (MLC, TLC, QLC).

Channel hot-electron injection

With high drain and gate voltages, a few electrons in the channel gain more than 3.1 eV near the drain and are injected over the barrier. Fast but inefficient (large programming current). Used in NOR flash.

Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling

A field above about 8–10 MV/cm thins the oxide barrier to a triangle that electrons tunnel through (WKB):

$$J_{FN}=AE_{ox}^2\,e^{-B/E_{ox}},\qquad B=\frac{4\sqrt{2m_{ox}}\,(q\phi_b)^{3/2}}{3q\hbar}.$$

Very low current per cell, so many cells are programmed in parallel. Used for program and erase in NAND flash and for erase in NOR.

Reliability. Retention is limited by leakage of charge through the tunnel oxide (it cannot be thinner than about 7–8 nm) and endurance by oxide damage accumulated in each program/erase cycle ($10^3$–$10^5$ cycles), which creates traps and stress-induced leakage. As cells shrink, a state is represented by fewer electrons (tens in the smallest planar cells), so the loss of a few electrons already shifts the threshold: one reason for the move to 3D charge-trap architectures.
