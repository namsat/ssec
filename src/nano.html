Chapter 11

Surfaces, heterostructures and low-dimensional systems

Devices are made of interfaces, and when their dimensions approach the electron wavelength the quantum mechanics of Chapter 02 becomes the device physics: confinement quantizes motion, conductance comes in units of $2e^2/h$, barriers are crossed by tunnelling, and single electrons can be counted.

Transport is diffusive when the sample is longer than the mean free path ($L\gg\ell$), ballistic when $L\ll\ell$, and quantum-coherent when $L < L_\varphi$. Confinement effects appear when a dimension is comparable to $\lambda_F=2\pi/k_F$: about 0.5 nm in metals but 30–50 nm in a semiconductor 2DEG, which is why semiconductors are the natural host for quantum devices.

2. Surface and interface effects

A surface breaks the translational symmetry on which Bloch's theorem rests. New solutions of the Schrödinger equation appear with complex wavevector, decaying both into the vacuum and into the crystal: surface states (Tamm and Shockley states), with energies that may lie in the bulk gap. On covalent semiconductors they originate from the broken (“dangling”) bonds, about $10^{15}$ cm⁻² of them; the surface lowers its energy by relaxation and reconstruction (for example Si(111) 7×7 and Si(100) 2×1), or by passivation with hydrogen or an oxide.

  • Work function and electron affinity. $\Phi=E_{vac}-E_F$ depends on the surface orientation and on adsorbates through the surface dipole; $\chi=E_{vac}-E_c$ for a semiconductor.
  • Band bending. Charge trapped in surface states is balanced by a space-charge layer below the surface, so the bands bend even on a free surface.
  • Fermi-level pinning. If the density of surface states is high (≳ $10^{13}$ cm⁻² eV⁻¹), small shifts of $E_F$ relative to them absorb any amount of charge: $E_F$ at the surface is locked near their charge-neutrality level $\phi_0$, about one third of the gap above $E_v$.
  • The Si–SiO₂ interface is the exception that made MOS technology possible: thermal oxidation followed by hydrogen annealing reduces the interface-trap density to $\sim10^{10}$ cm⁻² eV⁻¹.

Metal–semiconductor contacts

Schottky barrier height (n-type)
$$\text{Schottky–Mott (no interface states): }q\phi_{Bn}=q(\Phi_M-\chi),\qquad\text{Bardeen (pinned): }q\phi_{Bn}=E_g-q\phi_0,$$ $$\text{in general: }\phi_{Bn}=S\,(\Phi_M-\chi)+(1-S)\Big(\frac{E_g}{q}-\phi_0\Big),\qquad0\le S\le1 .$$

$S\approx1$ for ionic semiconductors and oxides; $S\approx0.1$–$0.3$ for Si and GaAs, where the barrier is nearly independent of the metal (about $\frac23E_g$ on n-type). The depletion layer is that of a one-sided junction, with $V_{bi}=\phi_{Bn}-V_T\ln(N_c/N_D)$. The current is carried by majority carriers emitted over the barrier (thermionic emission):

$$J=A^*T^2e^{-\phi_{Bn}/V_T}\big(e^{V/V_T}-1\big),\qquad A^*=\frac{4\pi qm^*k_B^2}{h^3}=120\,\frac{m^*}{m_0}\ \text{A cm}^{-2}\text{K}^{-2}.$$

Compared with a p–n diode the saturation current is orders of magnitude larger (lower turn-on voltage, 0.2–0.4 V) and there is no minority-carrier storage (fast switching). The image force lowers the barrier by $\Delta\phi=\sqrt{qE_{\max}/4\pi\varepsilon_s}$, which makes the reverse current rise with voltage.

Ohmic contacts. With heavy doping ($N_D\gtrsim10^{19}$ cm⁻³) the depletion layer is only a few nanometres thick and electrons tunnel through it (field emission): the contact resistance falls as $\rho_c\propto\exp\!\big(2\phi_{Bn}\sqrt{\varepsilon_sm^*}/\hbar\sqrt{N_D}\big)$. Every device terminal relies on this.

3. Heterojunctions and the two-dimensional electron gas

A heterojunction joins two semiconductors with different gaps. If the lattice constants match (GaAs/AlGaAs, InGaAs/InP, Si/SiGe under strain) the interface is nearly free of defects. The gap difference is shared between the bands as band offsets $\Delta E_c$ and $\Delta E_v$. Anderson's rule (alignment of vacuum levels) gives the first estimate, $\Delta E_c=q(\chi_1-\chi_2)$, but measured offsets are used in practice.

AlignmentDescriptionExample
Type I (straddling)the smaller gap lies inside the larger: electrons and holes confined in the same layerGaAs/AlGaAs, InGaAs/InP: lasers, LEDs
Type II (staggered)electrons and holes confined in different layersInP/InAlAs, Si/SiGe: detectors
Type III (broken gap)conduction band of one below the valence band of the otherInAs/GaSb: tunnel devices

For AlxGa1−xAs on GaAs with $x<0.45$: $E_g=1.424+1.247x$ eV, $\Delta E_c\simeq0.62\,\Delta E_g$, $\Delta E_v\simeq0.38\,\Delta E_g$.

Modulation doping. Dope only the wide-gap barrier. Electrons leave their donors and fall into the lower conduction band of the undoped narrow-gap layer, where they are held against the interface by the field of the ionized donors they left behind. They are confined in a roughly triangular well: motion perpendicular to the interface is quantized, motion in the plane is free. This is a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG):

$$E=E_i+\frac{\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)}{2m^*},\qquad E_i\simeq\Big(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\Big)^{1/3}\Big[\frac{3\pi qF}{2}\Big(i+\frac34\Big)\Big]^{2/3},\quad F=\frac{qn_s}{\varepsilon},\qquad E_F-E_0=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m^*}.$$

Because the electrons are separated from the donors (further helped by an undoped spacer layer), ionized-impurity scattering is suppressed: mobilities exceed $10^6$ cm²/Vs at low temperature in GaAs, giving mean free paths of many micrometres. The 2DEG is the platform of the HEMT, of the quantum Hall effects and of most mesoscopic experiments.

4. The high-electron-mobility transistor

A HEMT (or MODFET) is a field-effect transistor whose channel is a 2DEG. A Schottky gate on the doped barrier layer of thickness $d$ controls the sheet density like a capacitor:

$$n_s=\frac{\varepsilon}{q(d+\Delta d)}\,(V_G-V_{off}),\qquad V_{off}=\phi_B-\frac{\Delta E_c}{q}-\frac{qN_Dd^2}{2\varepsilon},$$

where $\Delta d\approx8$ nm accounts for the distance of the electrons from the interface. The sheet density saturates near $n_{s,\max}\approx\sqrt{2\varepsilon N_D\Delta E_c}/q$, beyond which carriers accumulate in the low-mobility barrier. With velocity saturation the drain current and transconductance are

$$I_{D,sat}\simeq qn_s(V_G)\,v_{sat}\,W,\qquad g_m\simeq\frac{\varepsilon v_{sat}W}{d+\Delta d},\qquad f_T\simeq\frac{v_{sat}}{2\pi L_g}.$$

The high mobility gives low access resistance and low noise; the high peak velocity of III–V materials gives $f_T$ of hundreds of gigahertz. AlGaN/GaN HEMTs obtain $n_s\approx10^{13}$ cm⁻² without any doping, from the polarization charge at the interface, and combine it with a breakdown field ten times that of silicon: the basis of modern RF power amplifiers and fast power converters.

5. Low-dimensional systems: density of states, wells and wires

Confinement in one, two or three directions gives quantum wells (2D), quantum wires (1D) and quantum dots (0D). The energy is the sum of quantized levels for the confined directions and a parabola for the free ones, and the density of states changes shape:

Density of states (including spin)
$$g_{3D}(E)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad g_{2D}(E)=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\sum_i\Theta(E-E_i),$$ $$g_{1D}(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\Theta(E-E_{ij})}{\sqrt{E-E_{ij}}},\qquad g_{0D}(E)=2\sum_{ijk}\delta(E-E_{ijk}).$$
Derivation

In $d$ free dimensions the allowed $\vb k$ have density $L^d/(2\pi)^d$. The number of states with wavevector below $k$, per unit ($d$-dimensional) volume and with spin, is $N_3=k^3/3\pi^2$, $N_2=k^2/2\pi$, $N_1=2k/\pi$. With $k=\sqrt{2m^*(E-E_i)}/\hbar$, $g=\dd N/\dd E$:

$$g_3=\frac{k^2}{\pi^2}\frac{\dd k}{\dd E}=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\Big(\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}\Big)^{3/2}\sqrt E,\qquad g_2=\frac{k}{\pi}\frac{\dd k}{\dd E}=\frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2},\qquad g_1=\frac2\pi\frac{\dd k}{\dd E}=\frac1\pi\sqrt{\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{E-E_i}} .$$

Each quantized level of the confined directions contributes one such term (a subband).

∎

The step-like 2D density of states is why quantum-well lasers have lower threshold and weaker temperature dependence than bulk lasers: carriers are not wasted filling states near the band edge. In quantum dots the spectrum is discrete, like an atom's.

Quantum wells

A thin layer of narrow-gap material between wide-gap barriers confines electrons and holes. Subbands are those of a finite well with the band offset as depth. The lowest optical transition is shifted above the bulk gap, $h\nu=E_g+E_{e1}+E_{hh1}$ (minus the exciton binding energy), so the emission colour is tuned by the layer thickness. Selection rule: $\Delta n=0$ for interband transitions in a symmetric well.

Quantum wires

With two confined directions only $k_x$ remains: each transverse mode $(i,j)$ is a one-dimensional subband. Wires are made by etching or gating a 2DEG, by epitaxial growth (nanowires), or occur naturally (carbon nanotubes). Their transport is described by the Landauer formula.

6. The Landauer formula

Consider a conductor connected to two reservoirs with electrochemical potentials $\mu_L$ and $\mu_R=\mu_L-qV$. There is no scattering inside except a transmission probability $T_n(E)$ per mode.

Landauer formula
$$I=\frac{2e}{h}\sum_n\int T_n(E)\,\big[f_L(E)-f_R(E)\big]\,\dd E\quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{linear response},\ T\to0\ }\quad G=\frac{2e^2}{h}\sum_nT_n(E_F).$$

The conductance quantum is $G_0=2e^2/h=77.5\ \mu$S, i.e. $1/G_0=12.9$ k$\Omega$.

Derivation

In one mode, right-moving states with wavevector in $\dd k$ carry a current $\dd I=e\,v(k)\,\frac{2\,\dd k}{2\pi}f_L$, where $2\,\dd k/2\pi$ is the density of states per unit length with spin. Since $v=\frac1\hbar\frac{\dd E}{\dd k}$, the velocity cancels the one-dimensional density of states:

$$\dd I=e\,\frac1\hbar\frac{\dd E}{\dd k}\,\frac{\dd k}{\pi}f_L=\frac{2e}{h}f_L(E)\,\dd E .$$

A fraction $T(E)$ is transmitted. Subtracting the current of left-movers injected from the right reservoir gives $I=\frac{2e}{h}\int T(E)[f_L-f_R]\dd E$. For small $V$ and low temperature $f_L-f_R\to eV\,\delta(E-E_F)$, so $I=\frac{2e^2}{h}T(E_F)V$. Summing over the occupied transverse modes gives the result.

∎

  • A perfect ($T_n=1$) conductor with $N$ modes has a finite resistance $h/2e^2N$. It is a contact resistance: electrons from the many modes of the wide reservoir must squeeze into the few modes of the wire.
  • The number of modes of a wire of width $W$ is $N=\mathrm{int}(k_FW/\pi)=\mathrm{int}(2W/\lambda_F)$.
  • For a long diffusive conductor $\sum T_n\approx N\ell/L$, which recovers Ohm's law $G=\sigma W/L$.
  • The multi-terminal version (Landauer–Büttiker) explains the quantum Hall effect, where edge channels have $T=1$ and the Hall resistance is $h/\nu e^2$ to parts in $10^{10}$.

Quantum point contact. A short constriction in a 2DEG, defined by negatively biased split gates, shows conductance steps of $2e^2/h$ as the gate voltage widens the channel and lets modes through one at a time (van Wees, Wharam, 1988). For a saddle-point potential the transmission of mode $n$ is $T_n(E)=\big[1+e^{-2\pi(E-E_n)/\hbar\omega_x}\big]^{-1}$.

7. Tunnelling through multiple barriers and resonant tunnelling

Two barriers enclosing a well form a Fabry–Pérot resonator for electron waves. Although each barrier alone transmits weakly ($T_1,T_2\ll1$), the total transmission reaches

$$T(E)\simeq\frac{4T_1T_2}{(T_1+T_2)^2}\,\frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E-E_r)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2},\qquad\Gamma\simeq\frac{\hbar v}{2w}(T_1+T_2),$$

which equals one at the resonance energy $E_r$ (the quasi-bound level of the well) if the barriers are equal. The width $\Gamma$ gives the lifetime of the level, $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$. With $N$ coupled wells each level splits into $N$ resonances; for many wells they merge into a miniband: an artificial crystal, or superlattice (Esaki and Tsu, 1970).

Resonant-tunnelling diode. Current flows when occupied emitter states are aligned with the level in the well. Increasing the bias pulls the level down through the emitter Fermi sea, and the current rises; when the level drops below the emitter conduction-band edge no state can tunnel while conserving energy and transverse momentum, and the current falls: negative differential resistance. The current follows from the Tsu–Esaki formula:

$$J=\frac{qm^*k_BT}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}\int_0^\infty T(E_z,V)\,\ln\!\left[\frac{1+e^{(E_F-E_z)/k_BT}}{1+e^{(E_F-E_z-qV)/k_BT}}\right]\dd E_z .$$

Because tunnelling is fast (sub-picosecond), RTDs oscillate above 1 THz. Sequential resonant tunnelling through tens of wells, with photon emission between subbands at each step, is the principle of the quantum cascade laser.

8. Coulomb blockade and the single-electron transistor

A small conducting island coupled to leads only through tunnel junctions holds an integer number of electrons. Adding one costs the charging energy

$$E_C=\frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma},\qquad C_\Sigma=C_1+C_2+C_g .$$

For a 100 nm island $C_\Sigma\sim10^{-17}$ F and $E_C\sim8$ meV; for a 2 nm island $E_C$ exceeds 0.5 eV. Single-electron effects are observable when:

Conditions for Coulomb blockade
  • $E_C\gg k_BT$: thermal fluctuations cannot supply the charging energy.
  • $R_T\gg h/e^2=25.8$ k$\Omega$: the junctions are opaque enough for the electron number to be well defined (from $\Delta E\,\Delta t\gtrsim\hbar$ with $\Delta t=R_TC$).

With $n$ excess electrons the electrostatic energy is $E(n)=(ne-C_gV_g)^2/2C_\Sigma$. At low bias, current is blocked unless two charge states are degenerate, $E(n)=E(n+1)$, which happens at $C_gV_g=(n+\frac12)e$. The conductance therefore shows Coulomb oscillations periodic in gate voltage with period $\Delta V_g=e/C_g$: each peak adds exactly one electron to the island. In the $(V_g,V_{ds})$ plane the blocked regions are the Coulomb diamonds, whose half-height is $e/C_\Sigma$. This is the single-electron transistor (SET).

The orthodox theory computes the current from tunnelling rates that depend on the change of free energy $\Delta F$ of each event,

$$\Gamma=\frac{1}{e^2R_T}\,\frac{-\Delta F}{1-e^{\Delta F/k_BT}},$$

and a master equation for the probabilities of the charge states. With very different junction resistances the $I$–$V_{ds}$ curve shows the Coulomb staircase, one step per additional electron. In semiconductor quantum dots the level spacing $\Delta\varepsilon$ adds to the charging energy (“artificial atoms” with shell filling). SETs are the most sensitive electrometers known ($\sim10^{-5}\,e/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$), serve as charge sensors for spin qubits, and single-electron pumps define the ampere through $I=ef$.

9. Two-dimensional materials

Layered crystals held together by van der Waals forces can be thinned to a single atomic layer. Graphene is a honeycomb lattice of carbon: a triangular Bravais lattice with two atoms (A, B) per cell. With one $p_z$ orbital per atom and nearest-neighbour hopping $t\approx2.8$ eV the tight-binding Hamiltonian is a 2×2 matrix:

$$H(\vb k)=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta/2&-tf(\vb k)\\-tf^*(\vb k)&-\Delta/2\end{pmatrix},\quad f(\vb k)=\sum_{j=1}^3e^{i\vb k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta_j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\pm(\vb k)=\pm\sqrt{t^2|f(\vb k)|^2+\Delta^2/4},$$ $$|f|^2=3+2\cos(\sqrt3k_ya)+4\cos\!\Big(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}k_ya\Big)\cos\!\Big(\frac32k_xa\Big),\qquad a=0.142\text{ nm}.$$

For equivalent sublattices ($\Delta=0$) the bands touch at the corners K, K′ of the hexagonal Brillouin zone, where $f=0$. Expanding around K:

Dirac cones
$$E_\pm(\vb q)=\pm\hbar v_F|\vb q|,\qquad v_F=\frac{3ta}{2\hbar}\approx10^6\ \text{m/s},\qquad g(E)=\frac{2|E|}{\pi\hbar^2v_F^2}.$$

Electrons behave as massless Dirac fermions with a pseudospin (the sublattice amplitude) locked to the direction of motion. Consequences: zero gap and a density of states that vanishes linearly at the neutrality point; ambipolar field effect with mobilities above $10^5$ cm²/Vs when encapsulated; suppression of backscattering and perfect transmission through barriers at normal incidence (Klein tunnelling); an anomalous quantum Hall effect with $\sigma_{xy}=\pm4(n+\frac12)e^2/h$. The missing gap prevents a graphene transistor from switching off, which motivates nanoribbons (Simulations), bilayers in an electric field, and other 2D crystals:

MaterialGapNotes
Graphene0semimetal, Dirac cones; interconnects, sensors, RF
Hexagonal BN≈ 6 eVsame lattice with inequivalent sublattices ($\Delta\ne0$); ideal substrate and gate dielectric
MoS₂, WS₂, MoSe₂, WSe₂1.6–2.0 eV (monolayer)indirect in bulk, direct in the monolayer; strong excitons (binding ≈ 0.5 eV), spin–valley coupling; transistors with $I_{on}/I_{off}>10^8$

An atomically thin channel has the shortest possible natural length (Chapter 10), hence excellent gate control at nanometre gate lengths. Stacking different layers gives van der Waals heterostructures without lattice-matching constraints; a small twist angle creates a moiré superlattice with flat bands (magic-angle bilayer graphene, 1.1°), which shows correlated insulating and superconducting phases.
