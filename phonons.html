Chapter 06

Phonons, optical properties and superconductivity

The ions of a crystal are not at rest. Their collective vibrations carry heat, scatter electrons, absorb infrared light and, at low temperature, glue electrons into pairs that flow without resistance.

1. Lattice vibrations

Expand the Born–Oppenheimer energy around equilibrium to second order in the displacements $\vb u$ (harmonic approximation): the crystal is a set of masses joined by springs.

Monatomic chain. Mass $M$, spring constant $C$, spacing $a$: $M\ddot u_n=C(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n)$. A travelling wave $u_n=Ae^{i(qna-\omega t)}$ gives

$$\omega(q)=2\sqrt{\frac CM}\,\Big|\sin\frac{qa}{2}\Big| .$$

For $qa\ll1$, $\omega=v_sq$ with sound velocity $v_s=a\sqrt{C/M}$; at the zone boundary the group velocity vanishes (standing wave). $q$ and $q+2\pi/a$ describe the same atomic motion, so $q$ lives in the first Brillouin zone.

Diatomic chain
$$\omega_\pm^2(q)=C\Big(\frac{1}{M_1}+\frac{1}{M_2}\Big)\pm C\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1}{M_1}+\frac{1}{M_2}\Big)^2-\frac{4\sin^2(qa/2)}{M_1M_2}} .$$
Derivation

Let $u_n$ and $w_n$ be the displacements of the two atoms in cell $n$ (period $a$):

$$M_1\ddot u_n=C(w_n+w_{n-1}-2u_n),\qquad M_2\ddot w_n=C(u_{n+1}+u_n-2w_n).$$

With $u_n=Ue^{i(qna-\omega t)}$, $w_n=We^{i(qna-\omega t)}$:

$$\begin{pmatrix}2C-M_1\omega^2&-C(1+e^{-iqa})\\-C(1+e^{iqa})&2C-M_2\omega^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\W\end{pmatrix}=0 .$$

The determinant gives $M_1M_2\omega^4-2C(M_1+M_2)\omega^2+2C^2(1-\cos qa)=0$, whose roots are the expression above since $1-\cos qa=2\sin^2(qa/2)$.

At $q=0$: the lower root has $\omega=0$ and $U=W$ (atoms move together: acoustic); the upper root $\omega^2=2C(1/M_1+1/M_2)$ has $M_1U=-M_2W$ (atoms move in opposition with fixed centre of mass: optical).

∎

In three dimensions a crystal with $p$ atoms per primitive cell has $3p$ branches: 3 acoustic (1 longitudinal LA, 2 transverse TA) and $3p-3$ optical (LO, TO). In ionic or polar crystals the optical modes carry an oscillating dipole and couple to infrared light. Silicon ($p=2$) has optical phonons at 64 meV at $\Gamma$; GaAs has LO at 36 meV and TO at 33 meV.

2. Phonons and crystal momentum

Each normal mode $(\vb q,s)$ is an independent harmonic oscillator. Quantizing as in Chapter 02:

$$\hat H=\sum_{\vb q,s}\hbar\omega_s(\vb q)\Big(\hat a^\dagger_{\vb qs}\hat a_{\vb qs}+\frac12\Big),\qquad\expval{n_{\vb qs}}=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega_s(\vb q)/k_BT}-1}.$$

A phonon is one quantum of a mode. Phonons are bosons with zero chemical potential, and their thermodynamics was derived in Statistics. The atomic displacement operator is $\hat{\vb u}(\vb R)=\sum_{\vb qs}\sqrt{\hbar/2NM\omega_s}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_s\big(\hat a_{\vb qs}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\vb qs}\big)e^{i\vb q\cdot\vb R}$.

Crystal momentum

$\hbar\vb q$ is not a mechanical momentum (a vibration with $\vb q\ne0$ carries no net momentum of the crystal), but it is conserved in every scattering process up to a reciprocal-lattice vector, because the crystal is invariant only under lattice translations:

$$\vb k_{in}=\vb k_{out}\pm\vb q+\vb G,\qquad E_{in}=E_{out}\pm\hbar\omega_s(\vb q).$$

These rules are the basis for measuring dispersion relations: inelastic neutron scattering maps $\omega_s(\vb q)$ over the whole zone because thermal neutrons have both the right energy and wavelength; Raman and Brillouin scattering of light reach only $q\approx0$ (optical and acoustic phonons respectively), since photon wavevectors are tiny on the scale of the zone.

3. Phonon–phonon scattering and thermal properties

In a perfectly harmonic crystal phonons would not interact, thermal expansion would vanish and the thermal conductivity would be infinite. Cubic and quartic terms in the potential (anharmonicity) let phonons decay and merge.

  • Thermal expansion. With $U(x)=cx^2-gx^3$, the mean displacement is $\expval x=3gk_BT/4c^2$. In general $\alpha_V=\gamma C_V/B$, with the Grüneisen parameter $\gamma=-\dd\ln\omega/\dd\ln V\approx1$–$2$.
  • Three-phonon processes. $\vb q_1+\vb q_2=\vb q_3+\vb G$. If $\vb G=0$ (normal process) the total crystal momentum of the phonon gas is unchanged and the heat flux is not degraded. If $\vb G\ne0$ (umklapp process) the resulting phonon is folded back into the zone with reversed group velocity: this is what produces thermal resistance.
Lattice thermal conductivity
$$\kappa_L=\frac13C_Vv_s\ell,\qquad\frac1\ell=\frac{1}{\ell_{boundary}}+\frac{1}{\ell_{defect}}+\frac{1}{\ell_{umklapp}} .$$
Kinetic derivation and temperature regimes

Phonons crossing a plane at $z$ last collided a distance $\ell$ away on average, where the energy density was $u(z\mp\ell\cos\theta)$. The net flux is $j=-\expval{v_z\ell\cos\theta}\frac{\dd u}{\dd z}=-\frac13v\ell\frac{\dd u}{\dd T}\frac{\dd T}{\dd z}$, so $\kappa=\frac13Cv\ell$.

High $T$ ($T>\theta_D$). The number of phonons is $\propto T$, so $\ell_U\propto1/T$ and $\kappa\propto1/T$ with $C_V$ constant.

Intermediate $T$. An umklapp event needs at least one phonon with $q\gtrsim G/4$, energy $\sim k_B\theta_D/2$; their number is $\propto e^{-\theta_D/bT}$, so $\ell_U\propto e^{\theta_D/bT}$ grows exponentially.

Low $T$. $\ell$ saturates at the sample size or defect spacing, and $\kappa\propto C_V\propto T^3$.

∎

4. Optical properties of matter

The linear response of a medium to light is contained in the complex dielectric function $\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_1+i\varepsilon_2$, or equivalently the complex refractive index $N=n+i\kappa=\sqrt{\varepsilon}$:

$$\varepsilon_1=n^2-\kappa^2,\quad\varepsilon_2=2n\kappa,\qquad E\propto e^{i\omega(Nz/c-t)}\ \Rightarrow\ I(z)=I_0e^{-\alpha z},\quad\alpha=\frac{2\omega\kappa}{c}=\frac{\omega\varepsilon_2}{nc},\qquad R=\frac{(n-1)^2+\kappa^2}{(n+1)^2+\kappa^2}.$$

Causality links the real and imaginary parts through the Kramers–Kronig relations: absorption at one frequency implies dispersion at all others. The equivalent optical conductivity is $\sigma(\omega)=-i\omega\varepsilon_0[\varepsilon(\omega)-1]$.

Lorentz oscillator and Drude limit
$$\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\qquad\omega_p^2=\frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m}.$$

Bound charges: $\omega_0\neq0$ (insulators, phonons). Free carriers: $\omega_0=0$ (Drude metal), $\gamma=1/\tau$.

Derivation

An electron bound harmonically and driven by $E_0e^{-i\omega t}$: $m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x+m\omega_0^2x=-eE_0e^{-i\omega t}$. The steady state is $x=\dfrac{-eE/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}$. With $n$ oscillators per volume the polarization is $P=-nex=\varepsilon_0\chi E$, hence $\varepsilon=1+\chi$. Contributions of higher-lying resonances are lumped into $\varepsilon_\infty$.

Drude metal ($\omega_0=0$, $\gamma\ll\omega$): $\varepsilon\simeq\varepsilon_\infty-\omega_p^2/\omega^2$ is negative below the screened plasma frequency $\omega_p/\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$: the wave is evanescent and $R\to1$. Above it the metal becomes transparent (ultraviolet transparency of alkali metals). In doped semiconductors the plasma edge lies in the infrared and measures $n/m^*$.

∎

Polar crystals. The TO phonon acts as a Lorentz oscillator: $\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty+\dfrac{(\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_\infty)\omega_{TO}^2}{\omega_{TO}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}$. Between $\omega_{TO}$ and $\omega_{LO}$ the dielectric function is negative and the crystal reflects totally (reststrahlen band), with the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation $\omega_{LO}^2/\omega_{TO}^2=\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_\infty$.

Direct and indirect gaps. A photon carries negligible crystal momentum ($2\pi/\lambda\ll\pi/a$), so optical transitions are vertical in the band diagram. Using Fermi's golden rule with the joint density of states:

$$\text{direct gap:}\quad\alpha(\hbar\omega)\propto\frac{\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_g}}{\hbar\omega},\qquad\text{indirect gap:}\quad\alpha\propto\frac{(\hbar\omega-E_g+\hbar\Omega)^2}{e^{\hbar\Omega/k_BT}-1}+\frac{(\hbar\omega-E_g-\hbar\Omega)^2}{1-e^{-\hbar\Omega/k_BT}} .$$

In an indirect semiconductor (Si, Ge) the conduction minimum is not at the same $\vb k$ as the valence maximum, and a phonon of energy $\hbar\Omega$ must be absorbed or emitted to conserve crystal momentum. The process is second order: absorption is weak near the edge and radiative recombination is inefficient, which is why LEDs and lasers use direct-gap materials (GaAs, InGaN) while silicon solar cells need a thickness of ~100 µm.

6. Electron–phonon interaction

A lattice displacement changes the potential felt by electrons. To first order, $\hat H_{e-ph}=\sum_{\vb k\vb q}g_{\vb q}\,\hat c^\dagger_{\vb k+\vb q}\hat c_{\vb k}\big(\hat a_{\vb q}+\hat a^\dagger_{-\vb q}\big)$: an electron is scattered from $\vb k$ to $\vb k+\vb q$ by absorbing or emitting a phonon. Consequences:

  • Resistivity of metals. The scattering rate is proportional to the number of thermal phonons: $\rho\propto T$ for $T\gg\theta_D$ and $\rho\propto T^5$ for $T\ll\theta_D$.
  • Mobility in semiconductors. Acoustic deformation-potential scattering gives $\mu\propto T^{-3/2}$; polar optical scattering dominates in GaAs at room temperature (Semiconductors).
  • Mass renormalization and polarons. The electron drags a cloud of lattice distortion: $m^*\to m^*(1+\lambda)$.
  • Effective attraction. An electron attracts nearby positive ions; the heavy ions respond slowly (time $\sim1/\omega_D$) and the region stays positively charged after the electron has left, attracting a second electron. Exchanging a virtual phonon gives an interaction $V_{\vb q}(\omega)=\dfrac{2|g_{\vb q}|^2\hbar\omega_{\vb q}}{(\hbar\omega)^2-(\hbar\omega_{\vb q})^2}$, which is attractive for energy transfers $|\hbar\omega|<\hbar\omega_{\vb q}$. Because it is retarded, it can overcome the screened Coulomb repulsion.
Cooper problem

Two electrons added above a filled Fermi sea with an arbitrarily weak attraction $-V$ acting within $\hbar\omega_D$ of $E_F$ form a bound state with binding energy

$$E_b\simeq2\hbar\omega_D\,e^{-2/N(0)V}.$$

The Fermi sea is unstable against pairing of states $(\vb k\uparrow,-\vb k\downarrow)$. The result is non-analytic in $V$ and cannot be found by perturbation theory.

7. Introduction to superconductivity

Phenomenology. Below a critical temperature $T_c$: (i) the dc resistance vanishes; (ii) magnetic flux is expelled from the bulk (Meissner effect), which distinguishes a superconductor from a perfect conductor; (iii) superconductivity is destroyed above a critical field $H_c(T)\simeq H_c(0)[1-(T/T_c)^2]$ or a critical current; (iv) the electronic heat capacity jumps at $T_c$ and falls exponentially below it, indicating a gap.

London equations and penetration depth
$$\frac{\partial\vb j_s}{\partial t}=\frac{n_se^2}{m}\vb E,\qquad\nabla\times\vb j_s=-\frac{n_se^2}{m}\vb B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nabla^2\vb B=\frac{\vb B}{\lambda_L^2},\qquad\lambda_L=\sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0n_se^2}}.$$
Derivation of the exponential screening

Superelectrons accelerate freely, $m\dot{\vb v}=-e\vb E$, which gives the first equation with $\vb j_s=-n_se\vb v$. Taking the curl and using Faraday's law, $\partial_t(\nabla\times\vb j_s+\frac{n_se^2}{m}\vb B)=0$. London postulated that the bracket itself vanishes, which encodes the Meissner effect. Combining with Ampère's law $\nabla\times\vb B=\mu_0\vb j_s$ and $\nabla\cdot\vb B=0$: $\nabla\times\nabla\times\vb B=-\nabla^2\vb B=-\mu_0\frac{n_se^2}{m}\vb B$. For a field parallel to a flat surface, $B(x)=B_0e^{-x/\lambda_L}$: field and screening currents exist only within $\lambda_L\sim20$–$200$ nm of the surface.

∎

BCS theory (Bardeen, Cooper, Schrieffer, 1957). The ground state is a coherent superposition of Cooper pairs $(\vb k\uparrow,-\vb k\downarrow)$. Main results in the weak-coupling limit:

$$k_BT_c=1.13\,\hbar\omega_D\,e^{-1/N(0)V},\qquad2\Delta(0)=3.53\,k_BT_c,\qquad\frac{N_s(E)}{N(0)}=\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}}\ \ (|E|>\Delta),\qquad\frac{\Delta C}{\gamma T_c}=1.43 .$$
  • Energy gap. Breaking a pair costs $2\Delta$; seen in tunnelling spectra, infrared absorption and the exponential heat capacity.
  • Isotope effect. $T_c\propto\omega_D\propto M^{-1/2}$: direct evidence that phonons mediate the pairing.
  • Coherence length. $\xi_0=\hbar v_F/\pi\Delta$ is the size of a pair, much larger than the spacing between electrons.
  • Flux quantization. The pair wavefunction must be single valued: flux through a ring is a multiple of $\Phi_0=h/2e=2.07\times10^{-15}$ Wb; the charge $2e$ confirms pairing.
  • Josephson effects. Across a thin barrier a supercurrent $I=I_c\sin\varphi$ flows at zero voltage, and a voltage $V$ makes it oscillate at $f=2eV/h$ (483.6 GHz/mV), the basis of SQUID magnetometers and of the voltage standard.

Type I and type II. The Ginzburg–Landau parameter $\kappa=\lambda/\xi$ decides the sign of the surface energy between normal and superconducting regions. For $\kappa<1/\sqrt2$ (type I: Al, Sn, Pb) the Meissner state collapses abruptly at $H_c$. For $\kappa>1/\sqrt2$ (type II: Nb, NbTi, Nb₃Sn, cuprates) flux enters above $H_{c1}$ as vortices each carrying $\Phi_0$, and superconductivity survives up to $H_{c2}=\Phi_0/2\pi\mu_0\xi^2$, which can reach tens of tesla. Pinned vortices allow lossless transport in high fields: this is what makes superconducting magnets possible.
