Chapter 09

The bipolar junction transistor

Two p–n junctions sharing a thin base make a transistor. The forward-biased emitter junction injects carriers into the base; almost all of them cross it and are collected by the reverse-biased collector junction. A small base current controls a large collector current, and the control is exponential in the base–emitter voltage.

All formulas are written for an npn transistor with uniform doping $N_E\gg N_B>N_C$, neutral base width $W_B$ and area $A$. For pnp exchange electrons with holes and reverse all voltages and currents.

1. How a BJT works

RegionB–E junctionB–C junctionBehaviour
Forward activeforwardreverse$I_C=\beta_FI_B$: amplifier
Saturationforwardforward$V_{CE}\approx0.1$–$0.2$ V: closed switch
Cut-offreversereverseonly leakage: open switch
Reverse activereverseforwardroles of E and C exchanged, low gain $\beta_R$

In forward-active operation three things happen. (1) The emitter junction injects electrons into the base; the number is set by the law of the junction, $n_B(0)=n_{B0}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$. (2) The base is much thinner than the diffusion length, so the electrons diffuse across with little recombination. (3) At the collector junction the field sweeps them into the collector whatever the value of $V_{CB}$: the collector behaves as a current source. The base current only has to supply the two loss mechanisms: holes back-injected into the emitter and holes recombining in the base.

Design rules that follow
  • Emitter much more heavily doped than base, so that the emitter-junction current is mostly electrons (high injection efficiency).
  • Thin base ($W_B\ll L_n$), so that few electrons recombine (high transport factor) and transit is fast.
  • Lightly doped collector, so that the collector depletion region extends into the collector, not the base, giving high breakdown voltage and small Early effect.

Derivation of the transport factor

In the neutral base $D_Bn''=(n-n_{B0})/\tau_B$ with $\Delta n(0)=n_{B0}(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1)\equiv\Delta_0$ and $\Delta n(W_B)\simeq0$ (reverse-biased collector junction). The solution is $\Delta n(x)=\Delta_0\sinh[(W_B-x)/L_B]/\sinh(W_B/L_B)$. The electron currents at the two ends are $qAD_B|\Delta n'|$:

$$I_{nE}=\frac{qAD_B\Delta_0}{L_B}\coth\frac{W_B}{L_B},\qquad I_{nC}=\frac{qAD_B\Delta_0}{L_B}\frac{1}{\sinh(W_B/L_B)},\qquad\alpha_T=\frac{I_{nC}}{I_{nE}}=\frac{1}{\cosh(W_B/L_B)}.$$

For $W_B\ll L_B$ the profile is a straight line, $I_{nE}\simeq qAD_B\Delta_0/W_B$, and $1-\alpha_T\simeq W_B^2/2L_B^2=\tau_t/\tau_B$, where $\tau_t=W_B^2/2D_B$ is the base transit time: the fraction lost equals transit time over lifetime.

∎

The quantity $G_B=\int_0^{W_B}N_B\,\dd x/D_B$ (the Gummel number) summarises the base: $I_C=qAn_i^2e^{V_{BE}/V_T}/G_B$. In modern transistors $\beta_F$ is limited by the injection efficiency, including band-gap narrowing in the heavily doped emitter; heterojunction bipolar transistors use a wider-gap emitter to suppress hole back-injection by $e^{\Delta E_g/k_BT}$.

3. Carrier concentrations in emitter, base and collector

The law of the junction applied at both junctions fixes the boundary values of the minority concentrations:

$$p_E(0^-)=p_{E0}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad n_B(0)=n_{B0}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad n_B(W_B)=n_{B0}\,e^{V_{BC}/V_T},\qquad p_C(W_B^+)=p_{C0}\,e^{V_{BC}/V_T}.$$

In a thin base the profile joins the two boundary values with a straight line:

$$n_B(x)\simeq n_{B0}\Big[e^{V_{BE}/V_T}\Big(1-\frac{x}{W_B}\Big)+e^{V_{BC}/V_T}\frac{x}{W_B}\Big],\qquad I_n=\frac{qAD_Bn_{B0}}{W_B}\big(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-e^{V_{BC}/V_T}\big).$$

The slope of the line gives the electron current through the base and the area under it gives the stored base charge $Q_B$, which controls the base recombination and the switching speed. Each operating region has a characteristic shape: a triangle with its high side at the emitter (forward active), a trapezoid (saturation), a slightly negative, nearly empty base (cut-off), a triangle with its high side at the collector (reverse active).

4. The Ebers–Moll equations

The minority-carrier equations are linear, so the solution for arbitrary $V_{BE},V_{BC}$ is the superposition of a forward component (driven by $V_{BE}$ with $V_{BC}=0$) and a reverse component (driven by $V_{BC}$ with $V_{BE}=0$).

Ebers–Moll equations (injection version)
$$\begin{aligned}I_E&=I_{ES}\big(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1\big)-\alpha_RI_{CS}\big(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\big)\\I_C&=\alpha_FI_{ES}\big(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1\big)-I_{CS}\big(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\big)\end{aligned}\qquad\qquad\alpha_FI_{ES}=\alpha_RI_{CS}\equiv I_S\ \ \text{(reciprocity)}.$$

Circuit: two diodes back to back, each shunted by a current source controlled by the current of the other diode.

Derivation and proof of reciprocity

Forward component ($V_{BC}=0$). The emitter junction is a diode with saturation current $I_{ES}=qA\big(\frac{D_Bn_{B0}}{W_B}+\frac{D_Ep_{E0}}{L_E^*}\big)$ carrying $I_F=I_{ES}(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1)$; a fraction $\alpha_F$ reaches the collector.

Reverse component ($V_{BE}=0$). The collector junction is a diode with $I_{CS}=qA\big(\frac{D_Bn_{B0}}{W_B}+\frac{D_Cp_{C0}}{L_C}\big)$ carrying $I_R=I_{CS}(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1)$; a fraction $\alpha_R$ reaches the emitter.

Superposing with the reference directions ($I_E$ out of the emitter, $I_C$ into the collector): $I_E=I_F-\alpha_RI_R$, $I_C=\alpha_FI_F-I_R$.

Reciprocity. $\alpha_FI_{ES}$ is the part of the forward diode current due to electrons crossing the base, $qAD_Bn_{B0}/W_B$ (times $\alpha_T$). $\alpha_RI_{CS}$ is the same quantity for the reverse direction. Both equal the electron transport coefficient of the base, which is symmetric: $I_S=qAD_Bn_i^2/N_BW_B$. Only three parameters are independent.

∎

The equivalent transport version, used in circuit simulators, takes the electron current through the base as the single controlled source:

$$I_{CT}=I_S\big(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-e^{V_{BC}/V_T}\big),\qquad I_C=I_{CT}-\frac{I_S}{\beta_R}\big(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\big),\qquad I_B=\frac{I_S}{\beta_F}\big(e^{V_{BE}/V_T}-1\big)+\frac{I_S}{\beta_R}\big(e^{V_{BC}/V_T}-1\big).$$

5. Reverse active, saturation and cut-off

Reverse active. The collector injects and the emitter collects. Because the collector is lightly doped, its injection efficiency is poor, and geometrically the emitter collects only part of the flow: $\beta_R\sim0.1$–$10$.

Saturation. Both junctions inject. The collector current is set by the external circuit and is less than $\beta_FI_B$; the excess base current maintains extra stored charge. From the Ebers–Moll equations:

$$V_{CE,sat}=V_{BE}-V_{BC}=V_T\ln\left[\frac{1}{\alpha_R}\cdot\frac{1+\dfrac{I_C}{I_B}\dfrac{1}{\beta_R+1}}{1-\dfrac{I_C}{\beta_FI_B}}\right].$$

Typical values are 50–200 mV plus the ohmic drop in the collector. Leaving saturation requires removing the stored charge (storage delay), which limits the speed of saturating logic; Schottky clamps between base and collector prevent deep saturation.

Cut-off. Both junctions reverse biased: $I_E\simeq-I_{ES}(1-\alpha_F)$ and $I_C\simeq I_{CS}(1-\alpha_R)$, of the order of picoamperes. With the base open, $I_{CE0}=(\beta_F+1)I_{CB0}$: leakage is amplified.

6. Static characteristics and second-order effects

Early effect (base-width modulation). Increasing the reverse bias on the collector junction widens its depletion region and shortens the neutral base. Since $I_C\propto1/W_B$:

$$I_C=I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}\Big(1+\frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\Big),\qquad V_A=W_B\Big/\Big|\frac{\dd W_B}{\dd V_{CB}}\Big|\simeq\frac{qN_BW_B}{C_{jC}/A},\qquad r_o=\frac{V_A}{I_C}.$$

A heavily doped base and a lightly doped collector give a high $V_A$. If the base is depleted completely the transistor punches through.

Breakdown. With the emitter open the collector junction breaks down at $BV_{CBO}$. In common emitter, carriers generated by multiplication act as base current and are amplified, so breakdown comes earlier: $BV_{CEO}\simeq BV_{CBO}/\beta_F^{1/m}$ with $m\approx3$–$6$.

Gain versus current: the Gummel plot. Plotting $\log I_C$ and $\log I_B$ against $V_{BE}$ shows where the ideal theory holds. $I_C$ follows 60 mV/decade over many decades. $I_B$ has an extra component from recombination in the emitter depletion region, $\propto e^{V_{BE}/2V_T}$, which lowers $\beta$ at low current. At high current, high-level injection in the base (Webster effect), base push-out (Kirk effect) and emitter crowding reduce $\beta$ again.

7. Small-signal model

Linearizing the forward-active equations around a bias point $(I_C,V_{CE})$ gives the hybrid-π circuit:

$$f_T=\frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)}=\frac{1}{2\pi\Big[\tau_F+\dfrac{V_T}{I_C}(C_{jE}+C_\mu)\Big]},\qquad\tau_F\simeq\frac{W_B^2}{2D_B}.$$
Derivation

With the output short-circuited, a base current $i_b$ develops $v_{be}=i_b/[1/r_\pi+j\omega(C_\pi+C_\mu)]$ and the collector current is $i_c\simeq g_mv_{be}$. The current gain is $\beta(\omega)=\dfrac{\beta_0}{1+j\omega r_\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu)}$, which falls to one at $\omega_T=g_m/(C_\pi+C_\mu)$ for $\beta_0\gg1$. Substituting $C_\pi=g_m\tau_F+C_{jE}$ and $g_m=I_C/V_T$ gives the second form: at low current the depletion capacitances dominate and $f_T\propto I_C$; at high current $f_T\to1/2\pi\tau_F$, the limit set by transit through the base.

∎

The intrinsic voltage gain $g_mr_o=V_A/V_T$ is independent of the bias current and reaches several thousand, the main advantage of bipolar over MOS devices in analog design.
