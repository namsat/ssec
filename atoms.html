Chapter 03

Atoms, spin and molecules

Rotational symmetry organises atomic physics. Angular-momentum algebra fixes the quantum numbers, the Coulomb potential fixes the energies, spin and the exclusion principle fix how levels are filled, and the same exchange symmetry that orders the helium spectrum also holds molecules together.

1. Spectrum and eigenstates of angular momentum

The orbital angular momentum $\hat{\vb L}=\hat{\vb r}\times\hat{\vb p}$ obeys $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=i\hbar\hat L_z$ and cyclic permutations. Any vector operator $\hat{\vb J}$ with these commutators is an angular momentum. Since $[\hat J^2,\hat J_z]=0$ we look for common eigenstates $\ket{j,m}$.

Theorem: quantization of angular momentum
$$\hat J^2\ket{j,m}=\hbar^2j(j+1)\ket{j,m},\qquad\hat J_z\ket{j,m}=\hbar m\ket{j,m},$$

with $j\in\{0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots\}$ and $m\in\{-j,-j+1,\dots,j\}$. The ladder operators $\hat J_\pm=\hat J_x\pm i\hat J_y$ act as $\hat J_\pm\ket{j,m}=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,\ket{j,m\pm1}$.

Proof from the commutation relations alone

Let $\hat J^2\ket{\lambda,m}=\hbar^2\lambda\ket{\lambda,m}$, $\hat J_z\ket{\lambda,m}=\hbar m\ket{\lambda,m}$. From $[\hat J_z,\hat J_\pm]=\pm\hbar\hat J_\pm$ and $[\hat J^2,\hat J_\pm]=0$, the state $\hat J_\pm\ket{\lambda,m}$ has the same $\lambda$ and $m\pm1$.

Since $\hat J^2-\hat J_z^2=\hat J_x^2+\hat J_y^2$ is non-negative, $\lambda\ge m^2$: the ladder must stop at some $m_{\max}=j$ and $m_{\min}$, where $\hat J_+\ket{\lambda,j}=0$ and $\hat J_-\ket{\lambda,m_{\min}}=0$. Using $\hat J_\mp\hat J_\pm=\hat J^2-\hat J_z^2\mp\hbar\hat J_z$:

$$0=\hat J_-\hat J_+\ket{\lambda,j}\Rightarrow\lambda=j(j+1),\qquad0=\hat J_+\hat J_-\ket{\lambda,m_{\min}}\Rightarrow\lambda=m_{\min}(m_{\min}-1).$$

Hence $m_{\min}=-j$. Going from $-j$ to $j$ in integer steps requires $2j$ to be a non-negative integer. The norm $\|\hat J_\pm\ket{j,m}\|^2=\hbar^2[j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)]$ gives the matrix elements.

∎

For orbital angular momentum, single-valuedness of the wavefunction restricts $l$ to integers. The eigenfunctions are the spherical harmonics $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)\propto P_l^m(\cos\theta)e^{im\varphi}$. Half-integer $j$ is realised by spin.

2. The two-body problem and the hydrogen atom

For two particles interacting through $V(|\vb r_1-\vb r_2|)$, the centre of mass $\vb R$ and the relative coordinate $\vb r=\vb r_1-\vb r_2$ separate:

$$\hat H=\frac{\hat P^2}{2M}+\frac{\hat p^2}{2\mu}+V(r),\qquad M=m_1+m_2,\quad\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$

The centre of mass moves freely; the internal motion is that of one particle of reduced mass $\mu$ in a central potential. Because $[\hat H,\hat L^2]=[\hat H,\hat L_z]=0$, write $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ and $u(r)=rR(r)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{\dd^2u}{\dd r^2}+\Big[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\Big]u=Eu .$$

This is a one-dimensional problem with a centrifugal barrier. For $V=-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$:

Hydrogen spectrum
$$E_n=-\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}\frac{1}{n^2}=-\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2},\qquad a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}=0.0529\ \text{nm},$$

$n=1,2,\dots$; $l=0,\dots,n-1$; $m=-l,\dots,l$. Each level is $n^2$-fold degenerate ($2n^2$ with spin).

$$R_{nl}(r)=\sqrt{\Big(\frac{2}{na_0}\Big)^3\frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n\,(n+l)!}}\;e^{-\rho/2}\rho^{\,l}L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho),\qquad\rho=\frac{2r}{na_0}.$$
Sketch of the solution of the radial equation

Use $\rho=2\kappa r$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{-2\mu E}/\hbar$. For $\rho\to\infty$, $u\sim e^{-\rho/2}$; for $\rho\to0$ the centrifugal term dominates and $u\sim\rho^{l+1}$. Write $u=\rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho/2}v(\rho)$; then $v$ satisfies

$$\rho v''+(2l+2-\rho)v'+(\nu-l-1)v=0,\qquad\nu=\frac{1}{\kappa a_0}.$$

A power series $v=\sum c_k\rho^k$ has $c_{k+1}/c_k=(k+l+1-\nu)/[(k+1)(k+2l+2)]$. Unless it terminates it behaves like $e^{\rho}$ and $u$ diverges. Termination at $k=n_r$ requires $\nu=n_r+l+1\equiv n$, hence $\kappa=1/na_0$ and $E_n=-\hbar^2/2\mu a_0^2n^2$. The polynomial solutions are the associated Laguerre polynomials.

∎

The same formulas describe hydrogenic systems after rescaling. For a shallow donor in a semiconductor replace $\mu\to m^*$ and $\varepsilon_0\to\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$: $E_d=13.6\,\text{eV}\cdot(m^*/m_0)/\varepsilon_r^2$ and $a^*=a_0\varepsilon_r/(m^*/m_0)$. In GaAs this gives 5.8 meV and 10 nm, the reason donors are fully ionized at room temperature. The same scaling gives the exciton binding energy.

3. Spin

The Stern–Gerlach experiment shows that an electron has an intrinsic angular momentum with only two orientations, $s=\tfrac12$, $m_s=\pm\tfrac12$. The state space is two-dimensional, spanned by $\ket\uparrow,\ket\downarrow$, and $\hat{\vb S}=\frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ with the Pauli matrices

$$\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k .$$

The full electron state is a spinor $\psi(\vb r)=\binom{\psi_\uparrow(\vb r)}{\psi_\downarrow(\vb r)}$. The spin state pointing along the unit vector $\hat n(\theta,\varphi)$ is $\ket{+_n}=\cos\frac\theta2\ket\uparrow+e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2\ket\downarrow$, so a measurement of $S_z$ gives $+\hbar/2$ with probability $\cos^2(\theta/2)$. The magnetic moment is $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=-g_s\mu_B\hat{\vb S}/\hbar$ with $g_s\approx2$ and $\mu_B=e\hbar/2m_e=5.788\times10^{-5}$ eV/T; in a field the spin precesses at the Larmor frequency $\omega_L=g_s\mu_BB/\hbar$.

4. Addition of angular momenta

For two angular momenta $\hat{\vb J}=\hat{\vb J}_1+\hat{\vb J}_2$ there are two natural bases: the product basis $\ket{j_1m_1}\ket{j_2m_2}$ and the coupled basis $\ket{j,m}$ of eigenstates of $\hat J^2,\hat J_z,\hat J_1^2,\hat J_2^2$.

Triangle rule
$$j=|j_1-j_2|,\ |j_1-j_2|+1,\ \dots,\ j_1+j_2,\qquad m=m_1+m_2,\qquad\sum_j(2j+1)=(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1).$$

The change of basis is given by the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, $\ket{j,m}=\sum_{m_1m_2}\braket{j_1m_1j_2m_2}{jm}\ket{j_1m_1}\ket{j_2m_2}$.

Two spins ½. $j=1$ (triplet, symmetric) and $j=0$ (singlet, antisymmetric):

$$\ket{1,1}=\ket{\uparrow\uparrow},\quad\ket{1,0}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\big),\quad\ket{1,-1}=\ket{\downarrow\downarrow},\qquad\ket{0,0}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\big).$$

Since $\hat{\vb S}_1\cdot\hat{\vb S}_2=\frac12(\hat S^2-\hat S_1^2-\hat S_2^2)$, it equals $+\hbar^2/4$ in the triplet and $-3\hbar^2/4$ in the singlet.

Spin–orbit coupling. $\hat H_{so}=\zeta\,\hat{\vb L}\cdot\hat{\vb S}/\hbar^2$ is diagonal in the coupled basis $j=l\pm\frac12$, with $\expval{\vb L\cdot\vb S}=\frac{\hbar^2}2[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\frac34]$. A $p$ level splits into $j=\frac32$ (energy $+\zeta/2$) and $j=\frac12$ (energy $-\zeta$). The same coupling splits off the valence band of semiconductors.

5. Time-independent perturbation theory

Let $\hat H=\hat H_0+\lambda\hat V$ with $\hat H_0\ket{n^{(0)}}=E_n^{(0)}\ket{n^{(0)}}$ known and non-degenerate.

Rayleigh–Schrödinger expansion
$$E_n=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda\bra{n^{(0)}}\hat V\ket{n^{(0)}}+\lambda^2\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{|\bra{m^{(0)}}\hat V\ket{n^{(0)}}|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}+\dots,\qquad\ket{n^{(1)}}=\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\bra{m^{(0)}}\hat V\ket{n^{(0)}}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\ket{m^{(0)}} .$$
Derivation

Insert $E_n=\sum_k\lambda^kE_n^{(k)}$ and $\ket n=\sum_k\lambda^k\ket{n^{(k)}}$ into $(\hat H_0+\lambda\hat V)\ket n=E_n\ket n$ and collect powers of $\lambda$:

$$\lambda^1:\quad(\hat H_0-E_n^{(0)})\ket{n^{(1)}}=(E_n^{(1)}-\hat V)\ket{n^{(0)}} .$$

Project on $\bra{n^{(0)}}$: the left side vanishes, giving $E_n^{(1)}=\bra{n^{(0)}}\hat V\ket{n^{(0)}}$. Project on $\bra{m^{(0)}}$, $m\ne n$: $(E_m^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})\braket{m^{(0)}}{n^{(1)}}=-V_{mn}$, which gives $\ket{n^{(1)}}$. At order $\lambda^2$, projecting on $\bra{n^{(0)}}$ gives $E_n^{(2)}=\bra{n^{(0)}}\hat V\ket{n^{(1)}}$.

∎

Second-order corrections to the ground state are always negative, and levels repel each other. If $E_n^{(0)}$ is degenerate the denominators vanish; one must first diagonalize $\hat V$ within the degenerate subspace (degenerate perturbation theory), whose eigenvalues are the first-order shifts. The gap at a Brillouin-zone boundary is exactly such a $2\times2$ problem.

6. Charged particles in an electromagnetic field; the Zeeman effect

With potentials $\vb B=\nabla\times\vb A$, $\vb E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\vb A$, the Hamiltonian of a charge $q$ follows from minimal coupling $\hat{\vb p}\to\hat{\vb p}-q\vb A$:

$$\hat H=\frac{(\hat{\vb p}-q\vb A)^2}{2m}+q\phi-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_s\cdot\vb B .$$

A gauge transformation $\vb A\to\vb A+\nabla\chi$, $\phi\to\phi-\partial_t\chi$ changes the wavefunction only by a phase $e^{iq\chi/\hbar}$, leaving all observables unchanged. For a uniform field choose $\vb A=\frac12\vb B\times\vb r$; expanding for an electron ($q=-e$):

$$\hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V(r)+\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\big(\hat{\vb L}+g_s\hat{\vb S}\big)\cdot\vb B+\frac{e^2}{8m}(\vb B\times\vb r)^2 .$$

The linear term is the paramagnetic (Zeeman) coupling; the quadratic term is diamagnetic and negligible for atoms in laboratory fields. Together with the spin–orbit term the atomic Hamiltonian is $\hat H_0+\zeta\,\hat{\vb L}\cdot\hat{\vb S}/\hbar^2+\mu_BB(\hat L_z+2\hat S_z)/\hbar$.

Weak field: $\mu_BB\ll\zeta$

$j,m_j$ are good quantum numbers. First-order perturbation theory with the Wigner–Eckart (projection) theorem gives

$$\Delta E=g_j\,\mu_BB\,m_j,\qquad g_j=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}$$

(the Landé factor; the “anomalous” Zeeman effect).

Strong field: $\mu_BB\gg\zeta$

$m_l,m_s$ are good quantum numbers (Paschen–Back regime):

$$\Delta E=\mu_BB\,(m_l+2m_s)+\zeta\,m_lm_s .$$

Optical lines form the “normal” triplet because $\Delta m_s=0$.

7. Identical particles, the exclusion principle and helium

Identical particles are indistinguishable: the Hamiltonian commutes with every exchange operator $\hat P_{ij}$, and physical states must be eigenstates of $\hat P_{ij}$.

Symmetrization postulate

States of identical particles are either totally symmetric (bosons, integer spin) or totally antisymmetric (fermions, half-integer spin) under exchange. For fermions occupying orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b,\dots$ the state is a Slater determinant:

$$\Psi(1,\dots,N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\big[\phi_{a_i}(j)\big].$$

Pauli exclusion principle: the determinant vanishes if two fermions occupy the same single-particle state.

Helium. $\hat H=\hat h_1+\hat h_2+\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12}}$ with hydrogenic $\hat h_i$ ($Z=2$). Neglecting the repulsion gives $E=-8\,\text{Ry}=-108.8$ eV; the measured value is $-79.0$ eV.

  • First-order perturbation theory: $\expval{e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12}}_{1s^2}=\frac54Z\,\text{Ry}=34$ eV, so $E\approx-74.8$ eV.
  • Variational method: use $1s$ orbitals with an effective charge $Z_e$ as trial function. In hartree, $E(Z_e)=Z_e^2-2ZZ_e+\tfrac58Z_e$, minimal at $Z_e=Z-\tfrac{5}{16}$: $E=-77.5$ eV. Each electron screens part of the nuclear charge seen by the other.

Excited states and exchange. For the configuration $1s\,nl$ the spatial part can be symmetric or antisymmetric, $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[\phi_{1s}(1)\phi_{nl}(2)\pm\phi_{nl}(1)\phi_{1s}(2)]$, and must be combined with the spin singlet or triplet respectively so that the total state is antisymmetric. The energies are

$$E_\pm=E_0+J\pm K,\qquad J=\iint|\phi_{1s}(1)|^2\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12}}|\phi_{nl}(2)|^2,\quad K=\iint\phi_{1s}^*(1)\phi_{nl}^*(2)\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12}}\phi_{nl}(1)\phi_{1s}(2).$$

The triplet (orthohelium) lies below the singlet (parahelium) by $2K$ because the antisymmetric spatial function keeps the electrons apart. Although the Hamiltonian contains no spin, the energy depends on the total spin: this is the exchange interaction, the origin of Hund's first rule and of ferromagnetism, often written as an effective Heisenberg coupling $-2K\,\hat{\vb S}_1\cdot\hat{\vb S}_2/\hbar^2$.

8. Many-electron atoms and electron–electron interaction

For $N$ electrons the repulsion terms $\sum_{i < j}e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{ij}$ prevent an exact solution. The central-field approximation replaces them by an average spherical potential: each electron moves in $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$, which tends to $-Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ near the nucleus and to $-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$ far away. Orbitals keep the labels $n,l,m_l,m_s$ but the $l$-degeneracy is lifted: for given $n$, low-$l$ orbitals penetrate the screening cloud and are more tightly bound ($E_{ns} < E_{np} < E_{nd}$).

  • Hartree method. Each orbital solves a Schrödinger equation in the electrostatic potential of all the others; the equations are iterated to self-consistency.
  • Hartree–Fock. Using a Slater determinant adds the non-local exchange term, which lowers the energy of parallel-spin pairs. What is left out is called correlation.
  • Aufbau and the periodic table. Shells fill in order of increasing $n+l$ (Madelung rule): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, … A subshell holds $2(2l+1)$ electrons.
  • Hund's rules for the ground term $^{2S+1}L_J$: (1) maximise $S$; (2) then maximise $L$; (3) $J=|L-S|$ if the shell is less than half full, $J=L+S$ otherwise.
Example: silicon

Si: $1s^22s^22p^63s^23p^2$. Two $p$ electrons: $S=1$, $L=1$, shell less than half full so $J=0$: ground term $^3P_0$. In the crystal the four valence electrons form $sp^3$ hybrids pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, the origin of the diamond structure.

9. Simple molecules and the exchange interaction

H₂⁺ (one electron, two protons). In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the nuclei are fixed at distance $R$. Take as trial function a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) $\psi_\pm=(\phi_a\pm\phi_b)/\sqrt{2(1\pm S)}$. In atomic units,

$$E_\pm(R)=E_{1s}+\frac1R-\frac{j\pm k}{1\pm S},\quad S=e^{-R}\Big(1+R+\frac{R^2}{3}\Big),\quad j=\frac1R-e^{-2R}\Big(1+\frac1R\Big),\quad k=e^{-R}(1+R).$$

The symmetric combination piles charge between the nuclei and is bonding; the antisymmetric one has a node at the midpoint and is antibonding. The splitting $\approx2k$ is the hopping energy $t$ of tight-binding theory: bringing $N$ atoms together turns each atomic level into a band of $N$ states.

H₂ (Heitler–London). With two electrons the spatial function $\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)\pm\phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)$ pairs with the spin singlet (+) or triplet (−). $E_\pm=2E_{1s}+\frac{Q\pm A}{1\pm S^2}$, where the exchange integral $A$ is negative: the singlet binds (covalent bond, opposite spins), the triplet repels.

10. Quantization of the electromagnetic field

In a cavity of volume $V$ expand the vector potential in normal modes $(\vb k,\lambda)$, $\lambda=1,2$ polarizations. The classical field energy is a sum of independent harmonic oscillators, $H=\sum_{\vb k\lambda}\frac12(P_{\vb k\lambda}^2+\omega_k^2Q_{\vb k\lambda}^2)$ with $\omega_k=ck$. Canonical quantization of each oscillator gives

$$\hat H=\sum_{\vb k\lambda}\hbar\omega_k\Big(\hat a^\dagger_{\vb k\lambda}\hat a_{\vb k\lambda}+\frac12\Big),\qquad\hat{\vb A}(\vb r)=\sum_{\vb k\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_0\omega_kV}}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_{\vb k\lambda}\big(\hat a_{\vb k\lambda}e^{i\vb k\cdot\vb r}+\hat a_{\vb k\lambda}^\dagger e^{-i\vb k\cdot\vb r}\big).$$

The quantum number $n_{\vb k\lambda}$ counts photons of energy $\hbar\omega_k$ and momentum $\hbar\vb k$; $\hat a^\dagger$ creates one, $\hat a$ destroys one. Photons are bosons, so any number can occupy a mode. Consequences:

  • Coupling an atom through $-\frac{q}{m}\hat{\vb A}\cdot\hat{\vb p}$, the matrix element for emitting into a mode containing $n$ photons is $\propto\sqrt{n+1}$: the rate has a part $\propto n$ (stimulated emission) and a part that survives at $n=0$ (spontaneous emission). Absorption is $\propto n$. These are Einstein's $B$ and $A$ coefficients, see Statistics.
  • The vacuum has zero-point fluctuations, responsible for the Lamb shift and the Casimir force.
  • Thermal occupation $\expval n=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1)$ gives Planck's law. Lattice vibrations are quantized in the same way and yield phonons.
