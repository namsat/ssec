Chapter 05

Crystals and energy bands

A crystal is a periodic arrangement of atoms. Periodicity alone, before any detail of the potential, forces electronic states into Bloch waves labelled by a crystal momentum and organises their energies into bands. This chapter goes from the full many-body Hamiltonian to the transport coefficients measured in the laboratory.

1. The Hamiltonian of a solid and its approximations

$$\hat H=\underbrace{\sum_I\frac{\hat P_I^2}{2M_I}}_{T_{ion}}+\underbrace{\sum_i\frac{\hat p_i^2}{2m}}_{T_e}+\underbrace{\frac12\sum_{I\ne J}\frac{Z_IZ_Je^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vb R_I-\vb R_J|}}_{V_{ii}}-\underbrace{\sum_{i,I}\frac{Z_Ie^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vb r_i-\vb R_I|}}_{V_{ei}}+\underbrace{\frac12\sum_{i\ne j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vb r_i-\vb r_j|}}_{V_{ee}}$$

With $\sim10^{23}$ coupled particles this cannot be solved directly. Three approximations make it tractable:

  1. Born–Oppenheimer (adiabatic). Since $m/M\sim10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}$, electrons adjust instantaneously to the ionic positions. Solve the electronic problem with ions fixed at $\{\vb R_I\}$; the resulting energy $E_{el}(\{\vb R_I\})$ acts as the potential for ionic motion, which yields phonons. The neglected coupling is the electron–phonon interaction.
  2. Valence and core. Tightly bound core electrons move with the nucleus; only valence electrons are treated explicitly, interacting with ion cores through a (pseudo)potential.
  3. Independent electrons (mean field). Replace $V_{ee}$ by an effective one-electron potential (Hartree, Hartree–Fock, or the Kohn–Sham potential of density-functional theory). Each electron then obeys $\big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+U(\vb r)\big]\psi=E\psi$ with $U$ periodic. Screening (Statistics) explains why this works as well as it does.

2. Direct and reciprocal lattice

A Bravais lattice is the set $\vb R=n_1\vb a_1+n_2\vb a_2+n_3\vb a_3$. A crystal structure is a lattice plus a basis of atoms attached to each point. There are 14 Bravais lattices in 3D; the cubic ones are simple (sc), body-centred (bcc) and face-centred (fcc). Silicon and GaAs are fcc with a two-atom basis at $(0,0,0)$ and $\frac a4(1,1,1)$ (diamond and zincblende). The Wigner–Seitz cell is the region closer to one lattice point than to any other.

Reciprocal lattice

The set of wavevectors $\vb G$ for which plane waves have the periodicity of the lattice, $e^{i\vb G\cdot\vb R}=1$. It is a Bravais lattice generated by

$$\vb b_1=2\pi\frac{\vb a_2\times\vb a_3}{\vb a_1\cdot(\vb a_2\times\vb a_3)}\ \ (\text{cyclic}),\qquad\vb a_i\cdot\vb b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}.$$
  • Any lattice-periodic function is a Fourier series over reciprocal vectors: $U(\vb r)=\sum_{\vb G}U_{\vb G}e^{i\vb G\cdot\vb r}$.
  • $\vb G=h\vb b_1+k\vb b_2+l\vb b_3$ is perpendicular to the lattice planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$, whose spacing is $d_{hkl}=2\pi/|\vb G|$. For cubic crystals $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$.
  • The reciprocal of fcc is bcc and vice versa. The Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice is the first Brillouin zone; its volume is $(2\pi)^3/V_{cell}$.
Proof that $\vb G_{hkl}$ is normal to the planes $(hkl)$ with $d=2\pi/|\vb G|$

The plane $(hkl)$ nearest the origin intercepts the axes at $\vb a_1/h,\vb a_2/k,\vb a_3/l$. Two vectors lying in the plane are $\vb a_1/h-\vb a_2/k$ and $\vb a_1/h-\vb a_3/l$. Using $\vb a_i\cdot\vb b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$, $\vb G\cdot(\vb a_1/h-\vb a_2/k)=2\pi-2\pi=0$, and likewise for the other, so $\vb G$ is normal to the plane. The distance from the origin to the plane is the projection of $\vb a_1/h$ on the unit normal: $d=\frac{\vb a_1}{h}\cdot\frac{\vb G}{|\vb G|}=\frac{2\pi}{|\vb G|}$.

∎

3. Crystals and X-ray diffraction

X-rays with $\lambda\sim0.1$ nm are scattered elastically by the electron density. Constructive interference requires:

Bragg law
$$2d_{hkl}\sin\theta=n\lambda$$

Reflection from parallel planes with path difference a multiple of $\lambda$.

Laue condition
$$\vb k'-\vb k=\vb G$$

The scattering vector is a reciprocal-lattice vector (Ewald construction).

Proof of the Laue condition and equivalence with Bragg

The amplitude scattered from identical scatterers at lattice sites is $\sum_{\vb R}e^{i(\vb k-\vb k')\cdot\vb R}$. The sum over $N$ sites is of order $N$ only if every phase is a multiple of $2\pi$, i.e. $\Delta\vb k=\vb k'-\vb k\in\{\vb G\}$; otherwise the terms cancel.

Elastic scattering has $|\vb k'|=|\vb k|=2\pi/\lambda$, and $\vb k'$ makes an angle $2\theta$ with $\vb k$, so $|\Delta\vb k|=2k\sin\theta$. Setting this equal to $|\vb G|=2\pi n/d$ gives $2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$.

∎

With a basis of atoms at $\vb d_j$ with form factors $f_j$, the intensity of the reflection $\vb G$ is proportional to $|S_{\vb G}|^2$, where the structure factor is $S_{\vb G}=\sum_jf_je^{-i\vb G\cdot\vb d_j}$. In the conventional cubic cell this gives the selection rules:

StructureAllowed reflectionsSequence of $h^2+k^2+l^2$
scall1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, …
bcc$h+k+l$ even2, 4, 6, 8, 10, …
fcc$h,k,l$ all odd or all even3, 4, 8, 11, 12, …
diamondfcc rule, and if all even then $h+k+l=4n$3, 8, 11, 16, 19, …

4. Cohesive energy

The cohesive energy is the energy needed to separate the crystal into neutral free atoms. Its size reflects the type of bonding.

BondingMechanismEnergy per atomExamples
van der Waalsfluctuating dipoles, $-C/r^6$0.02–0.2 eVNe, Ar, molecular crystals, layers of 2D materials
IonicCoulomb attraction of closed-shell ions6–9 eV per pairNaCl, MgO
Covalentshared electron pairs in directed bonds3–7 eVC, Si, Ge, GaAs
Metallicdelocalized electrons lower kinetic energy1–5 eV (up to 9 for W)Na, Cu, Al
Hydrogenproton shared between electronegative atoms0.1–0.5 eVice

Noble-gas crystals. With the Lennard-Jones pair potential $u(r)=4\epsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^6]$, summing over the fcc lattice gives the energy per atom

$$\frac UN=2\epsilon\Big[A_{12}\Big(\frac\sigma R\Big)^{12}-A_6\Big(\frac\sigma R\Big)^{6}\Big],\quad A_{12}=12.13,\ A_6=14.45\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_0=1.09\,\sigma,\quad\frac{U_0}{N}=-8.6\,\epsilon .$$

Ionic crystals. $\dfrac{U}{N_{pairs}}=-\dfrac{\alpha e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}+\dfrac{B}{R^n}$, with the Madelung constant $\alpha=\sum_j(\pm)R/r_j$ ($1.7476$ for NaCl, $1.7627$ for CsCl). Minimising: $U_0=-\dfrac{\alpha e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R_0}\big(1-\frac1n\big)$, which gives 7.9 eV per pair for NaCl ($R_0=0.282$ nm, $n\approx8$), within a few percent of experiment.

5. Translation symmetry and Bloch's theorem

Bloch's theorem

For $\hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+U(\vb r)$ with $U(\vb r+\vb R)=U(\vb r)$, eigenstates can be chosen as

$$\psi_{n\vb k}(\vb r)=e^{i\vb k\cdot\vb r}u_{n\vb k}(\vb r),\quad u_{n\vb k}(\vb r+\vb R)=u_{n\vb k}(\vb r)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\psi_{n\vb k}(\vb r+\vb R)=e^{i\vb k\cdot\vb R}\psi_{n\vb k}(\vb r).$$
Proof with translation operators

Define $\hat T_{\vb R}f(\vb r)=f(\vb r+\vb R)$. Because $U$ is periodic and $\nabla^2$ is translation invariant, $[\hat T_{\vb R},\hat H]=0$; also $\hat T_{\vb R}\hat T_{\vb R'}=\hat T_{\vb R+\vb R'}=\hat T_{\vb R'}\hat T_{\vb R}$. A set of mutually commuting operators has common eigenstates: $\hat H\psi=E\psi$, $\hat T_{\vb R}\psi=c(\vb R)\psi$.

The eigenvalues satisfy $c(\vb R+\vb R')=c(\vb R)c(\vb R')$ and, since $\hat T$ is unitary, $|c|=1$. Hence $c(\vb a_i)=e^{2\pi ix_i}$ and $c(\vb R)=e^{2\pi i(n_1x_1+n_2x_2+n_3x_3)}=e^{i\vb k\cdot\vb R}$ with $\vb k=x_1\vb b_1+x_2\vb b_2+x_3\vb b_3$. Defining $u=e^{-i\vb k\cdot\vb r}\psi$ one checks $u(\vb r+\vb R)=u(\vb r)$.

∎

  • Crystal momentum. $\hbar\vb k$ is not the momentum ($\psi_{n\vb k}$ is not an eigenstate of $\hat{\vb p}$) but it is conserved modulo $\hbar\vb G$. $\vb k$ and $\vb k+\vb G$ label the same state, so $\vb k$ can be restricted to the first Brillouin zone and $E_n(\vb k+\vb G)=E_n(\vb k)$.
  • Counting. Born–von Kármán periodic boundary conditions over $N$ cells allow exactly $N$ values of $\vb k$ in the zone, with density $V/(2\pi)^3$. Each band holds $2N$ electrons.
  • Band index. For each $\vb k$ the equation for $u_{n\vb k}$ in one cell has a discrete set of solutions $n=1,2,\dots$: the energy bands $E_n(\vb k)$.
  • Central equation. Expanding $\psi=\sum_{\vb G}c_{\vb k+\vb G}e^{i(\vb k+\vb G)\cdot\vb r}$: $\ \big[\frac{\hbar^2|\vb k+\vb G|^2}{2m}-E\big]c_{\vb k+\vb G}+\sum_{\vb G'}U_{\vb G-\vb G'}c_{\vb k+\vb G'}=0$, a matrix eigenvalue problem for each $\vb k$ (solved live in Simulations).

6. Nearly free electrons

For a weak potential the free-electron states $\ket{\vb k}$ are a good starting point, except where two of them, $\vb k$ and $\vb k-\vb G$, are degenerate: $|\vb k|=|\vb k-\vb G|$, the Bragg condition. There the potential mixes them strongly.

Gap at a Bragg plane
$$E_\pm(\vb k)=\frac{\varepsilon_{\vb k}+\varepsilon_{\vb k-\vb G}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\Big(\frac{\varepsilon_{\vb k}-\varepsilon_{\vb k-\vb G}}{2}\Big)^2+|U_{\vb G}|^2},\qquad E_{gap}=2|U_{\vb G}| .$$
Derivation by degenerate perturbation theory

Keep only the two coefficients $c_{\vb k}$ and $c_{\vb k-\vb G}$ in the central equation:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{\vb k}-E&U_{\vb G}\\U_{\vb G}^*&\varepsilon_{\vb k-\vb G}-E\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{\vb k}\\c_{\vb k-\vb G}\end{pmatrix}=0 .$$

The determinant gives $E_\pm$. On the Bragg plane $\varepsilon_{\vb k}=\varepsilon_{\vb k-\vb G}$ and $E_\pm=\varepsilon_{\vb k}\pm|U_{\vb G}|$, with eigenvectors $c_{\vb k}=\pm c_{\vb k-\vb G}$: in one dimension $\psi_+\propto\cos(\pi x/a)$ and $\psi_-\propto\sin(\pi x/a)$. The two standing waves have the same kinetic energy but pile charge either on the ions or between them, hence different potential energy. Away from the plane, to second order, $E\approx\varepsilon_{\vb k}+|U_{\vb G}|^2/(\varepsilon_{\vb k}-\varepsilon_{\vb k-\vb G})$: levels repel.

∎

7. Tight binding, group velocity and effective mass

The opposite limit starts from atomic orbitals $\phi(\vb r-\vb R)$. The Bloch sum $\psi_{\vb k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\vb R}e^{i\vb k\cdot\vb R}\phi(\vb r-\vb R)$ satisfies Bloch's theorem by construction. Keeping the on-site energy $\varepsilon_0$ and the nearest-neighbour hopping integral $-t=\bra{\phi(\vb r)}\hat H\ket{\phi(\vb r-\boldsymbol\delta)}$:

$$E(\vb k)=\varepsilon_0-t\sum_{\boldsymbol\delta}e^{i\vb k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta}\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \text{1D chain: }E=\varepsilon_0-2t\cos ka,\qquad\text{simple cubic: }E=\varepsilon_0-2t(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za).$$

The bandwidth ($4t$ in 1D, $12t$ in sc) grows with the overlap between neighbours: core levels give narrow bands, valence orbitals wide ones. A wave packet built from states near $\vb k$ moves with the group velocity and responds to forces with the effective mass tensor:

$$\vb v_n(\vb k)=\frac1\hbar\nabla_{\vb k}E_n(\vb k),\qquad\Big(\frac{1}{m^*}\Big)_{ij}=\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2E_n}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}.$$

Near a band minimum $E\simeq E_c+\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$ with $m^*=\hbar^2/2ta^2$ for the chain: strong overlap means light carriers. Near a maximum the curvature is negative.

8. Semiclassical dynamics, holes, metals and insulators

Semiclassical equations of motion
$$\dot{\vb r}=\vb v_n(\vb k)=\frac1\hbar\nabla_{\vb k}E_n,\qquad\hbar\dot{\vb k}=-e\big[\vb E+\vb v_n(\vb k)\times\vb B\big].$$

The band index is conserved (no interband transitions) as long as fields are weak and slowly varying. The periodic potential is fully included through $E_n(\vb k)$.

A filled band carries no current; definition of a hole

The current density of a band is $\vb j=-e\int_{occ}\frac{2\,\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\vb v(\vb k)$. For a full band the integral runs over the whole zone, and $\int_{BZ}\nabla_{\vb k}E\,\dd^3k=0$ because $E(\vb k)$ is periodic in $\vb k$. A uniform force shifts all $\vb k$ rigidly, but states leaving through one zone face re-enter through the opposite one: the band stays full and $\vb j=0$.

For a nearly full band, $\vb j=-e\int_{occ}\vb v=-e\big[\int_{BZ}-\int_{empty}\big]\vb v=+e\int_{empty}\frac{2\,\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\vb v(\vb k)$: the current equals that of positive charges $+e$ occupying the empty states. Near a band maximum $m^*<0$; an empty state there responds to a field as a particle of charge $+e$ and positive mass $m_h=-m^*$. This quasi-particle is the hole.

∎

  • Insulator / semiconductor: all bands either full or empty; $E_F$ lies in a gap. Requires an even number of electrons per primitive cell. Semiconductors are insulators with a gap small enough ($\lesssim3$ eV) to be thermally excited or doped.
  • Metal: at least one partially filled band (odd electron count, or overlapping bands as in the divalent metals).
  • Bloch oscillations: under a constant field $k$ grows linearly and the velocity oscillates with period $2\pi\hbar/eEa$. In ordinary crystals scattering intervenes long before; in superlattices the oscillation is observable.

9. Electrical transport in metals and insulators

The distribution function $f(\vb r,\vb k,t)$ obeys the Boltzmann equation. In the relaxation-time approximation collisions restore equilibrium at a rate $1/\tau$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\vb v\cdot\nabla_{\vb r}f-\frac{e}{\hbar}\vb E\cdot\nabla_{\vb k}f=-\frac{f-f_0}{\tau}.$$

For a uniform static field, to first order $f=f_0+e\tau\,\vb E\cdot\vb v\,(-\partial f_0/\partial E)$, which gives

$$\sigma=e^2\int\frac{2\,\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\tau\,v_x^2\Big(-\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial E}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{parabolic band}\ }\ \sigma=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m^*}=ne\mu,\qquad\mu=\frac{e\tau}{m^*}.$$

Only states within $k_BT$ of $E_F$ contribute, yet the result looks like Drude's formula with all $n$ electrons: the Fermi sphere is displaced rigidly by $\delta\vb k=-e\vb E\tau/\hbar$.

  • Matthiessen's rule: $1/\tau=1/\tau_{ph}+1/\tau_{imp}+\dots$, hence $\rho(T)=\rho_0+\rho_{ph}(T)$ with $\rho_{ph}\propto T$ above $\theta_D$ and $\propto T^5$ well below (Bloch–Grüneisen).
  • Hall effect: with $\vb B\perp\vb j$ a transverse field $E_y=R_Hj_xB$ builds up, $R_H=-1/ne$ for electrons and $+1/pe$ for holes. It measures the sign and density of carriers.
  • Thermal conductivity and Wiedemann–Franz law: $\kappa_{el}=\frac13C_{el}v_F\ell$ and $\dfrac{\kappa_{el}}{\sigma T}=L_0=\dfrac{\pi^2}{3}\Big(\dfrac{k_B}{e}\Big)^2=2.44\times10^{-8}$ W Ω K⁻².
  • Insulators: a full band has $\sigma=0$ at $T=0$; conduction requires thermally excited or injected carriers, $\sigma=e(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$.

10. Thermoelectric phenomena

With both an electric field and a temperature gradient, the linear response is

$$\vb j=\sigma(\vb E-S\nabla T),\qquad\vb j_Q=\Pi\,\vb j-\kappa\nabla T .$$
  • Seebeck effect: in open circuit a voltage $\Delta V=-S\Delta T$ appears. Carriers diffuse from the hot to the cold end; $S$ is negative for electrons and positive for holes.
  • Peltier effect: a current carries heat $\Pi\,\vb j$; heat is absorbed or released at a junction between two materials.
  • Thomson effect and Kelvin relations: $\Pi=ST$ and $\mu_T=T\,\dd S/\dd T$, consequences of Onsager reciprocity.
Mott formula (metals) and non-degenerate limit
$$S=-\frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{k_B^2T}{e}\Big[\frac{\dd\ln\sigma(E)}{\dd E}\Big]_{E_F}\sim-\frac{k_B}{e}\frac{k_BT}{E_F}\quad(\sim\mu\text{V/K}),\qquad S_n=-\frac{k_B}{e}\Big[\frac{E_c-E_F}{k_BT}+r+\frac52\Big]\quad(\sim\text{mV/K}).$$

The Seebeck coefficient is the entropy carried per unit charge: small in a degenerate metal, large in a lightly doped semiconductor. The efficiency of a thermoelectric generator or cooler depends on the figure of merit

$$ZT=\frac{S^2\sigma}{\kappa_{el}+\kappa_L}T .$$

Raising the carrier density increases $\sigma$ and $\kappa_{el}$ but lowers $S$, so $ZT$ peaks for heavily doped semiconductors around $10^{19}$–$10^{20}$ cm⁻³ (Bi₂Te₃, PbTe, SiGe).

The electrical properties of semiconductors, where carrier density is a design variable, are the subject of Chapter 07.
